### Attempts at relativistic quantum mechanics

The state of a system is represented by a vector in Hilbert space. Observables are Hermitian operators, and measurement returns one of their eigenvalues. Time evolution is governed by

$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\psi,t\rangle = H|\psi,t\rangle.
$$

For a spinless, non-relativistic particle with no forces,

$$
H = \frac{1}{2m}\mathbf{P}^2,
$$

so in the position basis

$$
i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\psi(x,t) = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi(x,t).
$$

The obvious relativistic replacement is

$$
H = +\sqrt{\mathbf{P}^2c^2 + m^2c^4}.
$$

Expanding in inverse powers of $c$ returns the rest energy and the non-relativistic kinetic term:

$$
H = mc^2 + \frac{1}{2m}\mathbf{P}^2 + \cdots
$$

The square root is awkward. Squaring before application gives the Klein–Gordon equation:

$$
-\hbar^2\frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2}\psi = \left(-\hbar^2c^2\nabla^2 + m^2c^4\right)\psi.
$$

### The conceptual snag

The Klein–Gordon equation allows negative-energy solutions. In a single-particle theory those solutions have no stable interpretation. The transition to quantum field theory begins when the field, rather than the particle wave function, becomes the fundamental object.
