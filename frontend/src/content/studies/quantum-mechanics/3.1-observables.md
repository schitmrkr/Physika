### Hermitian Operators

The expectation value of an observables $Q(x, p)$ can be expressed very neatly in the inner product notation.

$$
\langle Q \rangle = \int \Psi^* \hat{Q} \Psi dx = \langle \Psi, \hat{Q} \Psi \rangle
$$

The outcome of a mesurement has to be real,

$$
\langle Q \rangle = \langle Q \rangle^*
$$

So,

$$
\langle \Psi, \hat{Q} \Psi \rangle = \langle \hat{Q} \Psi, \Psi \rangle
$$

We call such operators hermitians. It has even more stronger condition than this: 

$$
\langle f, \hat{Q} g \rangle = \langle \hat{Q} f, g \rangle
$$

**Is momentum operator hermitian?**

$$
\langle f, \hat{p} g \rangle = \int_{-\infin}^{+\infin} f^* (-i \hbar) \frac{dg}{dx} dx
$$

$$
= -i \hbar f^* g |_{-\infin}^{+\infin} + \int_{-\infin}^{+\infin} (-i \hbar \frac{df}{dx})^* gdx = \langle \hat{p} f, g \rangle
$$

### Determinant States

The standard deviation of $Q$, in a determinate state, would be zero,

$$
\sigma^2 = \langle (Q - \langle Q \rangle)^2 \rangle = \langle \Psi | (\hat{Q} - q)^2 \Psi \rangle = \langle (\hat{Q} - q) \Psi | (\hat{Q} - q) \Psi \rangle = 0
$$

Which means that

$$
\hat{Q} \Psi = q \Psi
$$

Determinate states of $Q$ are eigenfunctions of $\hat{Q}$. Determinate states of the total energy are eigenfunctions of the Hamiltonian.

$$
\hat{H} \psi = E \psi
$$

### Eigenfunctions of a Hermitian operator

The collection of all eigenvalues of an operator is called its spectrum. If the spectrum is discrete then the eigenfunctions lie in Hilbert space and they constitute physically realizable states. 

If the spectrum is continuous then the eigenfuntions are not normalizable, they do not represent possible wave function.

### Discrete spectra

**Theorem 1: The normalizable eigenfunctions of a hermitian operator have real eigenvalues.**

Suppose,

$$
\hat{Q}f = qf
$$

and

$$
\langle f|\hat{Q}f \rangle = \langle \hat{Q}f|f \rangle
$$

i.e. $\hat{Q}$ is hermitian. Then,

$$
q \langle f|f \rangle = q^* \langle f|f \rangle
$$

Since, $q = q^*$, $q$ has to be real.


**Theorem 2: Eigenfunctions belonging to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.** 

Suppose,

$$
\hat{Q}f = qf, \qquad \hat{Q}g = q'g
$$

and $\hat{Q}$ is a hermitian. Then,

$$
\langle f|\hat{Q}g \rangle = \langle \hat{Q}f|g \rangle \\
q'\langle f|g \rangle = q^* \langle f|g \rangle
$$

Since, $q' \ne q$, then $\langle f|g \rangle = 0$. 


### Generalized Statistical Interpretation

If you measure an observable $Q(x,p)$ on a particle in the state $\Psi(x, t)$, you are certain to get one of the eigenvalues of the hermitian operator $\hat{Q}(x, -i\hbar d/dx)$. 

If the spectrum of $\hat{Q}$ is discrete, the probability of getting particular eigenvalue $q_n$ associated with the eigenfunction $f_n(x)$ is

$$
|c_n|^2, \qquad c_n = \langle f_n|\Psi \rangle
$$

If the spectrum is continuous, with real eigenvalue $q(z)$ associated with eigenfunction $f_z(x)$, the probability of getting a result in range $dz$ is

$$
|c(z)|^2, \qquad c(z) = \langle f_z|\Psi \rangle
$$

Upon measurement, the wave function collapses to corresponding eigenstate.

The eigenfunction of an observable operator are complete, so wavefunction can be written as linear combination.

$$
\Psi(x, t) = \sum_n c_n(t) f_n(x)
$$

Because eigenfunctions are orthonormal, the coefficients are given by Fourier's trick

$$
c_n(t) = \langle f_n|\Psi \rangle = \int f_n(x)^* \Psi(x, t) dx 
$$

Since total probability has to be one.

$$
\sum_n |c_n|^2 = 1
$$
