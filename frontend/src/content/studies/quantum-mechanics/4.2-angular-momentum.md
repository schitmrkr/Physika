### Angular Momentum

Hydrogen atoms are labeled by three quantum number, $n$, $l$, and $m$. The principle quantum number $n$ determines the energy of the state; $l$ and $m$ are related to the orbital angular momentum. 

Angular momentum in 2D can be expressed as:

$$
L_z = x p_y - y p_x
$$

This can be generalized as components of 3D angular momentum vector as

$$
\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}
$$

$$
\mathbf{L} =\begin{bmatrix}
\hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\
x & y & z \\
p_x & p_y & p_z
\end{bmatrix}
$$

which is to say

$$
L_x = y p_z - z p_u, \qquad L_y = z p_x - x p_z, \qquad L_z = x p_y - y p_x
$$

The corresponding quantum operators are obtained by the standard prescription,

$$
p_x \to -i\hbar \partial/\partial x, \qquad p_y \to -i\hbar \partial/\partial y, \qquad p_z \to -i\hbar \partial/\partial z
$$

Now we hope to obtain the eigenvalues of the angular momentum operator.

### Commutation Relations

The operators $L_x$ and $L_y$ do not commute. 

$$
[L_x, L_y] = [y p_z - z p_y, z p_x - x p_z] \\
= [y p_z, z p_x] - [y p_z, x p_z] - [z p_y, z p_x] + [z p_y, x p_z]
$$

In the equation $[L_x, L_y]$, consider the first term

$$
[y p_z, z p_x] 
$$

We know that,

$$
[AB, C] = A[B, C] + [A, C]B
$$

So,

$$
[y p_z, z p_x] = y[p_z,z p_x] + [y, z p_x] p_z = y[p_z,z p_x] = y [p_z​, z]p_x ​+ y z[p_z​, p_x​] \\
= y[p_z​, z]p_x = -i \hbar y p_x
$$

Also the second and third,

$$
[y p_z, x p_z] = [z p_y, z p_x] = 0
$$

Now for the fourth term,

$$
[z p_y, x p_z] = x[z, p_z]p_y = i \hbar x p_y
$$

Therefore,

$$
[L_x, L_y] = i \hbar (xp_y - yp_x) = i \hbar L_z
$$

Similarly,

$$
[L_x, L_y] = i \hbar L_z, \qquad [L_y, L_z] = i \hbar L_x, \qquad [L_z, L_x] = i \hbar L_y
$$

These are fundamental commutation relations for angular momentum; everything follows from them. 

Notice that $L_x$, $L_y$ and $L_z$ are incompatible observables. Incompatibility means that we cannot prepare a quantum state in which both components have perfectly definite values simultaneously.


$$
\sigma^2_{A} \sigma^2_{B} \ge \frac{1}{2} | \langle [A, B] \rangle |
$$

$$
\sigma^2_{L_x} \sigma^2_{L_y} \ge (\frac{1}{2i} \langle i \hbar L_z \rangle)^2 = \frac{\hbar^2}{4} \langle L_z \rangle^2
$$

or,

$$
\sigma_{L_x} \sigma_{L_y} \ge \frac{\hbar}{2} \left| \langle L_z \rangle \right|
$$

the square of total angular momentum

$$
L^2 \equiv L_x^2 + L_y^2 + L_z^2
$$

does commute with $L_x$,

$$
[L^2, L_x] = [L_x^2, L_x] + [L_y^2, L_x] + [L_z^2, L_x] \\
= 0 + L_y^2 L_x - L_x L_y^2 + L_z^2 L_x - L_x L_z^2 \\
= L_y L_y L_x - L_y L_x L_y + L_y L_x L_y - L_x L_y L_y +\\  L_z L_z L_x - L_z L_x L_z + L_z L_x L_z - L_x L_z L_z \\
= L_y[L_y, L_x] + [L_y, L_x]L_y + L_z[L_z, L_x] + [L_z, L_x]L_z \\
= L_y (-i\hbar L_z) + (-i\hbar L_z) L_y + L_z(i\hbar L_y) + (i\hbar Ly) L_z \\
= 0
$$

It follows:

$$
[L^2, L_x] = 0, \qquad [L^2, L_y] = 0, \qquad [L^2, L_z] = 0
$$

or 

$$
[L^2, \mathbf{L}] = 0
$$


### Ladder Operation

So, $L^2$ is compatible with each component of $\mathbf{L}$, we can hope to get simultaneous eigenstates of $L^2$ and (say) $L_z$:

$$
L^2 f = \lambda f, \qquad L_z f = \mu f
$$

We'll use ladder operator technique,

$$
L_{\pm} \equiv L_x \pm i L_y
$$

Its commutator with $L_z$ is

$$
[L_z, L_{\pm}] = [L_z, L_x] \pm i [L_z, L_y] = i\hbar L_y \pm i(-i\hbar L_x) = \pm \hbar (L_x \pm i L_y)
$$

so,

$$
[L_z, L_{\pm}]  = \pm \hbar L_\pm
$$

Also,

$$
[L^2, L_\pm] = 0
$$

***

**Claim: if $f$ is eigenfunction of $L^2$ and $L_z$, then so is $L_\pm f$.**

We start with the commutation relation,

$$
[L^2, L_\pm] = 0
$$

$$
L^2 (L_\pm f) = L_\pm (L^2 f)
$$

$$
L^2 (L_\pm f) = L_\pm(\lambda f) = \lambda (L_\pm f)
$$

$L_\pm f$ is an eigenfunction of $L^2$ with the same eigenvalue $\lambda$, 

$$
L_z (L_\pm f) = (L_z L_\pm - L_\pm L_z)f + L_\pm L_z f
$$

$$
L_z (L_\pm f) = \pm \hbar L_\pm + L_\pm (\mu f)
$$

$$
L_z (L_\pm f) = (\mu \pm \hbar) (L_\pm f)
$$

So, $L_\pm f$ is an eigenfunction of $L_z$ with the new eigenvalue $\mu \pm \hbar$. 

***

We call $L_+$ the raising operator, because it increases the the eigenvalue of $L_z$ by $\hbar$, and $L_-$ the lowering operater decreses the eigenvalue of $L_z$ by $\hbar$. 

For a given value of $\lambda$, we obtain a ladder of states, with each "rung" seperated by $\hbar$ in the eigenvalue of $L_z$. To ascend we use the raising operator and vice versa. But this process cannot go on forever. Eventually we are going to reach a state for which the $z$ component exceeds total and that cannot be. There must exist a "top rung" $f_t$ such that

$$
L_+ f_t = 0
$$

Let $\hbar l$ be eigenvalue of $L_z$ at the top rung

$$
L_z f_t = \hbar l f_t; \space L^2 f_t = \lambda f_t
$$

Now,

$$
L_\pm L_\mp = (L_x \pm iL_y)(L_x \mp iL_y)\\
= L_x^2 + L_y^2 \mp i(L_x L_y - L_y L_x) \\ 
= L^2 - L_z^2 \mp i (i\hbar L_z)
$$

or

$$
L^2 = L_\pm L_\mp + L_z^2 \mp \hbar L_z
$$

It follows that,

$$
L^2 f_t = (L_- L_+ + L_z^2 + \hbar L_z) f_t \\
= (0 + \hbar^2 l^2 + \hbar^2 l) f_t = \hbar^2 l(l+1) f_t
$$

Hence,

$$
\lambda = \hbar^2 l(l+1)
$$

Meanwhile there is also a bottom rung, $f_b$, such that

$$
L_- f_b = 0
$$

Simlarly, we get

$$
L^2 f_b = \hbar^2 \bar{l} (\bar{l} - 1) f_b
$$

Therfore,

$$
\lambda = \hbar^2 \bar{l} (\bar{l} - 1)
$$

So either $\bar{l} = l + 1$ or else $\bar{l} = - l$.

So the eigenvalues of $L_z$ are $m\hbar$, where $m$ goes from $-l$ to $l$ in $N$ integer steps. In particular it follows that $l = -l+N$, hence $l = N/2$, so $l$ must be an integer or half integer. 

The eighenfunctions are characterized by the number $l$ and $m$,

$$
L^2 f^m_l = \hbar^2 l(l+1) f^m_l
$$

$$
L_z f^m_l = \hbar m f^m_l
$$

where,

$$
l = 0, 1/2, 1, 3/2, ..;
$$

$$
m = -l, -l+1, ...., l-1, l;
$$

### What is $m$?

First of all we need to rewrite $L_x$, $L_y$ and $L_z$ in spherical coordinates. $\mathbf{L} = -i\hbar (\mathbf{r} \times \nabla)$ and the gradiant in spherical coordinates is,

$$
\nabla = \hat{r} \frac{\partial}{\partial r} + \hat{\theta} \frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} + \hat{\phi} \frac{1}{r \sin \theta} \frac{\partial}{\partial \phi}
$$

meanwhile, $\mathbf{r} = r \hat{r}$, so

$$
\mathbf{L} = -i\hbar [r (\hat{r} \times \hat{r}) \frac{\partial}{\partial r} + (\hat{r} \times \hat{\theta}) \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} + (\hat{r} \times \hat{\phi}) \frac{1}{\sin \theta} \frac{\partial}{\partial \phi}]
$$

$$
\mathbf{L} = -i\hbar [\hat{\phi} \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} + \hat{\theta} \frac{1}{\sin \theta} \frac{\partial}{\partial \phi}]
$$

Now when we resolve for $L_z$,

$$
L_z = -i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial \phi}
$$

So, if $f$ is an eigenfunction of $L_z$,

$$
L_z f = -i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial \phi} f = \lambda f
$$

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial \phi} = \frac{i \lambda}{\hbar} f
$$

$$
f(\phi) = A e^{i\lambda \phi/\hbar}
$$

Now we know that 

$$
\phi = 2 \pi + \phi
$$

So,

$$
A e^{i\lambda \phi/\hbar} = A e^{i\lambda (\phi + 2\pi)/\hbar}
$$

$$
e^{i \lambda 2 \pi / \hbar} = 1
$$

Therefore, 

$$
\lambda = m \hbar, \space m \in Z
$$

### What is $l$?

Now we look for eigenfunctions that satisfy, 

$$
L^2 Y = \lambda_L Y
$$

$$
L_z Y = m \hbar Y
$$

The remarkable result is that

$$
L^2 Y^m_l = \hbar^2 l(l+1) Y^m_l
$$

$$
L_z Y^m_l = m \hbar Y^m_l
$$

So, 

$$
L^2 \to \hbar^2 l(l+1)
$$

Here from previous derivations we know that $l = 0,1,2,...$. So for $m$.

$$
|\mathbf{L}| = \sqrt{l(l+1)} \hbar
$$

Meanwhile,

$$
L_z = m \hbar
$$

Also,

$$
|L_z| \le |\mathbf{L}|
$$

So roughly,

$$
m \hbar \le \sqrt{l(l+1)} \hbar
$$
