### From Gauge Invariance to Quantum Electrodynamics (QED)

We will combine the previous ideas into a Lagrangian describing matter interacting with electromagnetism.

We first use a complex scalar field $\psi(x)$, rather than an electron field, because its mathematics is simpler.

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{matter}} = \partial_\mu \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi - m^2 \psi^* \psi
$$

- $\partial_\mu\psi^*\partial^\mu\psi$ is the kinetic term: it describes how the field varies in spacetime
- $-m^2\psi^*\psi$ is the mass term: it encodes the field's mass in its equation of motion

The corresponding action is

$$
S = \int d^4x \space \mathcal{L}_{\text{matter}}
$$

The dynamics follow from the principle of stationary action, $\delta S=0$.

**Why does this Lagrangian have global $U(1)$ symmetry?**

Consider the global transformation $\psi' = e^{i q \alpha} \psi$, where $\alpha$ is a constant. Then,

$$
\psi'^* = e^{- i q \alpha} \psi^*
$$

Because $\alpha$ is constant, differentiation gives

$$
\partial_\mu \psi' = e^{i q \alpha} \partial_\mu \psi
$$

Therefore,

$$
\partial_\mu \psi'^* \partial^\mu \psi' = \partial_\mu \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi
$$

and

$$
\psi'^* \psi' = \psi^* \psi
$$

Both terms are invariant, so $\mathcal{L}'_{\text{matter}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{matter}}$

The key is that the phase factors cancel between $\psi$ and $\psi^*$.

But if $\alpha=\alpha(x)$, differentiating the field also differentiates the phase. The ordinary derivative is no longer covariant. We need the covariant derivative.


**Replace the ordinary derivative with the covariant derivative**

Our covariant derivative is

$$
D_\mu \psi = (\partial_\mu + iq A_\mu) \psi
$$

Its defining property is

$$
D'_\mu \psi' = e^{i q \alpha(x)} D_\mu \psi
$$

The complex conjugate transforms oppositely:

$$
(D_\mu \psi)'^* = e^{- i q \alpha(x)} (D_\mu \psi)^*
$$

Consequently, the product is invariant:

$$
(D_\mu \psi)'^* (D^\mu \psi)' = (D_\mu \psi)^* (D^\mu \psi)
$$

So we can construct a locally gauge-invariant matter Lagrangian simply by replacing ordinary derivatives with covariant derivatives:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{matter}} = (D_\mu \psi)^* (D^\mu \psi) - m^2 \psi^* \psi
$$

This is the minimally coupled scalar-field Lagrangian.


**Include the dynamics of the gauge field**

The matter Lagrangian alone does not describe the dynamics of electromagnetism. We also need a term for the gauge field:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{gauge}} = - \frac{1}{4} F_{\mu \nu} F^{\mu \nu}
$$

This is invariant under the local $U(1)$ transformation because $F_{\mu\nu}$ itself is gauge invariant.

Combining the two gives

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{L} = (D_\mu \psi)^* (D^\mu \psi) - m^2 \psi^* \psi - \frac{1}{4} F_{\mu \nu} F^{\mu \nu}}
$$

This is a gauge-invariant theory of a complex scalar field interacting with a $U(1)$ gauge field.



**Derive the Noether current**

Now we will connect global phase symmetry to a conserved quantity.

Noether's theorem says that a continuous global symmetry of the action gives a conserved current, provided the fields obey their equations of motion.

Make an infinitesimal transformation. For a small constant $\alpha$,

$$
\psi' = (1 + i q \alpha) \psi + O(\alpha^2)
$$

Thus the field variation is

$$
\delta \psi = i q \alpha \psi, \qquad \delta \psi^* = - i q \alpha \psi^*
$$

Use the Noether-current formula. For our Lagrangian, the current associated with this transformation is

$$
j^\mu = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \psi)} \frac{\delta \psi}{\alpha} + \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \psi^*)} \frac{\delta \psi^*}{\alpha}
$$

The derivatives of the Lagrangian with respect to the field derivatives are

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \psi)} = \partial^\mu \psi^*, \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \psi^*)} = \partial^\mu \psi
$$

Substituting the variations,

$$
j^\mu = i q (\psi \partial^\mu \psi^* - \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi)
$$

This is the Noether current for the global phase transformation under our chosen sign convention.

**Why is the current conserved?**

The Euler–Lagrange equations for the free complex scalar field are

$$
\partial_\mu \partial^\mu \psi + m^2 \psi = 0
$$

and

$$
\partial_\mu \partial^\mu \psi^* + m^2 \psi^* = 0
$$

Take the divergence of the current:

$$
\partial_\mu j^\mu = i q (\psi \Box \psi^* - \psi^* \Box \psi)  
$$

where $\Box = \partial_\mu \partial^\mu$. The terms containing first derivatives cancel. Using the equations of motion, $\Box \psi = - m^2 \psi$ and $\Box \psi^* = - m^2 \psi^*$, we obtain

$$
\partial_\mu j^\mu = i q (-m^2 \psi \psi^* + m^2 \psi^* \psi) = 0
$$

Therefore,

$$
\partial_\mu j^\mu = 0
$$

This is a local continuity equation.

**Interpret the conservation law**

Write $j^\mu = (\rho, \mathbf{j})$, with the time component interpreted as a charge density and the spatial components as a current density, under the chosed normalization. Then

$$
\partial_t \rho + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{j} = 0
$$

The equation says that charge cannot simply appear or disappear locally: a decrease in charge density inside a region must be accounted for by current flowing out of it.

The associated conserved quantity is

$$
Q = \int d^3x \space j^0
$$

If the current falls off sufficiently rapidly at spatial infinity, so there is no net flux through the boundary, then $dQ/dt=0$.

**Important distinction:** global symmetry gives the conserved Noether current; its conservation uses the equations of motion. Local gauge invariance is a different statement. It is a redundancy in how we describe the fields, not simply another name for a conserved current.

**Expand the covariant derivative: where the interaction lives**

Let's expand the scalar-field Lagrangian to see exactly what the gauge field does.

$$
D_\mu \psi = \partial_\mu \psi + iq A_\mu \psi
$$

and

$$
(D_\mu \psi)^* = \partial_\mu \psi^* - iq A_\mu \psi^*
$$

Multiplying these expressions gives

$$
(D_\mu \psi)^* (D^\mu \psi) = \partial_\mu \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi \\
+ iq A_\mu (\psi \partial^\mu \psi^* - \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi) \\
+ q^2 A_\mu A^\mu \psi^* \psi
$$

The full Lagrangian is therefore

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{free}} + A_\mu j^\mu + q^2 A_\mu A^\mu |\psi|^2 - \frac{1}{4} F_{\mu \nu} F^{\mu \nu}}
$$

where $j^\mu = iq (\psi \partial^\mu \psi^* - \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi)$ is the free matter Noether current.

The expansion reveals three important pieces.

**1. Free Matter**

$$
\partial_\mu \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi - m^2 |\psi|^2
$$

The matter field propagates without electromagnetic interactions.

**2. Linear Interaction**

$$
A_\mu j^\mu
$$

The gauge field couples to the matter current. This is the direct analogue of a field coupling to a source.

**3. Quadratic interaction**

$$
q^2 A_\mu A^\mu |\psi|^2
$$

This extra interaction is required by the covariant kinetic term for a charged scalar. It is often called the seagull term.

The quadratic term is especially revealing. Once we replace $\partial_\mu$ by $D_\mu$, gauge invariance determines both the linear and quadratic interactions in this minimally coupled scalar theory. We cannot keep the linear term and arbitrarily discard the quadratic one while claiming to retain the original covariant kinetic term.

The matter current that sources the gauge field is correspondingly

$$
J^\mu = iq [\psi (D^\mu \psi)^* - \psi^* D^\mu \psi]
$$

Expanding it gives $J^\mu = j^\mu + 2 q^2 A^\mu |\psi|^2$. Varying the full Lagrangian with respect to $A_\mu$ yields

$$
\partial_\mu F^{\mu \nu} = J^\nu
$$

This is the electromagnetic field equation in our conventions, with the current generated by the scalar matter field.



#### From scalar gauge theory to actual QED

The scalar theory taught us the structure of minimal coupling. But electrons are not described by scalar fields: they are spin-$\tfrac12$ particles, described relativistically by Dirac spinor fields.

Let $\Psi(x)$ be a Dirac field and let $\bar{\Psi} = \Psi^\dagger \gamma^0$ denote its Dirac adjoint. The free Dirac Lagrangian is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dirac}} = \bar{\Psi}(i \gamma^\mu \partial_\mu - m) \Psi
$$

Here, the gamma matrices $\gamma^\mu$ encode the relativistic spinor structure.

We apply exactly the same gauge principle:

$$
\Psi' = e^{iq\alpha(x)} \Psi, \qquad D_\mu = \partial_\mu + iq A_\mu
$$

The gauge-invariant Dirac matter Lagrangian is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dirac}} = \bar{\Psi} (i \gamma^\mu D_\mu - m) \Psi
$$

Now substitute $D_\mu$,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dirac}} = \bar{\Psi} (i \gamma^\mu (\partial_\mu + iq A_\mu) - m) \Psi \\
\mathcal{L}_{\text{Dirac}} = \bar{\Psi} (i \gamma^\mu \partial_\mu - m) \Psi - q \bar{\Psi} \gamma^ \mu A_\mu \Psi
$$

Adding the electromagnetic field term gives the QED Lagrangian:

**Quantum Electrodynamics (QED)**

$$
\boxed{\mathcal{L}_{\text{QED}} = \bar{\Psi}(i \gamma^\mu D_\mu - m) \Psi - \frac{1}{4} F_{\mu \nu} F^{\mu \nu}} 
$$

Equivalently, when expanded,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{QED}} = \bar{\Psi} (i \gamma^\mu \partial_\mu - m) \Psi \\
- q \bar{\Psi} \gamma^ \mu \Psi A_\mu  - \frac{1}{4} F_{\mu \nu} F^{\mu \nu}
$$

The interaction is the term

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}} = - q \bar{\Psi} \gamma^\mu \Psi A_\mu
$$

It couples the electromagnetic potential to the Dirac current $\bar\Psi\gamma^\mu\Psi$. The coefficient $q$ sets the coupling strength; for an electron, the electric charge is negative, so its sign must be handled consistently with the chosen convention.
