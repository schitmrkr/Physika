### Field Strength, Curvature, and Maxwell's Equations

Convention:

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{Object} & \text{Definition} \\
\hline
\text{Metric} & \eta_{\mu \nu} = \text{diag}(+1, -1, -1, -1) \\
\text{Natural units} & \hbar = c = 1 \\
\text{Matter transformation} & \psi' = e^{iq\alpha(x)} \psi \\
\text{Covariant derivative} & D_\mu = \partial_\mu + iq A_\mu \\
\text{Gauge transformation} & A'_\mu = A_\mu - \partial_\mu \alpha \\
\text{Field strength} & F_{\mu \nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu + \partial_\nu A_\mu \\
\end{array}
$$

#### Commutators

For ordinary partial derivatives acting on a sufficiently smooth function $f(x)$,

$$
[\partial_\mu, \partial_\nu]f = (\partial_\mu \partial_\nu - \partial_\nu \partial_\mu)f = 0
$$

The mixed derivatives commute.

But the covariant derivative is not merely an ordinary derivative. It includes the gauge potential:

$$
D_\mu = \partial_\mu + i q A_\mu
$$

Consequently, applying covariant derivatives in different orders may give different results:

$$
D_\mu D_\nu \psi \ne D_\nu D_\mu \psi
$$

We define their commutator as

$$
[D_\mu, D_\nu] \psi \equiv D_\mu (D_\nu \psi) - D_\nu (D_\mu \psi)
$$

The resulting difference will reveal the field strength.

Deriving the commutator step by step, we are left with

$$
[D_\mu, D_\nu] \psi = i q (\partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu) \psi
$$

This motivates the definition

$$
F_{\mu \nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu
$$

Hence the desired identity follows:

$$
[D_\mu, D_\nu] \psi = i q F_{\mu \nu} \psi
$$


#### $F_{\mu\nu}$ - Curvature

We have obtained the field strength through a commutator. But why does this have a geometric interpretation?

Think of $A_\mu$ as a connection: it tells us how to compare the internal phase of a field at neighboring spacetime points. The covariant derivative performs this comparison while accounting for the local gauge convention.

The commutator

$$
[D_\mu, D_\nu]
$$

measures the failure of covariant differentiation to commute. For our $U(1)$ theory, it is proportional to $F_{\mu\nu}$.

This is the gauge-theoretic analogue of curvature in differential geometry.


#### Prove that the field strength is gauge invariant

Recall

$$
A'_\mu = A_\mu - \partial_\mu \alpha
$$

Substitute it into the definition of the field strength:

$$
F'_{\mu \nu} = \partial_\mu A'_\nu - \partial_\nu A'_\mu
$$

$$
F'_{\mu \nu} = \partial_\mu (A_\nu - \partial_\nu \alpha) - \partial_\nu (A_\mu - \partial_\mu \alpha)
$$

$$
F'_{\mu \nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu - \partial_\mu \partial_\nu \alpha + \partial_\nu \partial_\mu \alpha
$$

For a sufficiently smooth $\alpha(x)$, the mixed derivatives commute. The last two terms cancel, giving

$$
F'_{\mu \nu} = F_{\mu \nu}
$$

Thus, the field strength is gauge invariant.

This has a clear interpretation:

- The potential $A_\mu$ can change under a gauge transformation.
- The curvature $F_{\mu\nu}$ does not change.
- Gauge-related potentials therefore encode the same local electromagnetic field.

#### From an antisymmetric tensor to electromagnetism

The field strength $F_{\mu\nu}$ is a rank-two antisymmetric tensor:

$$
F_{\mu \nu} = - F_{\nu \mu}
$$

Because its indices each take four values, it has $4 \times 4 = 16$ components as a matrix, but antisymmetry leaves only six independent components.

Those six components correspond to the three electric-field components and the three magnetic-field components.

Using the metric signature $(+,-,-,-)$, let

$$
A^\mu = (\phi, \mathbf{A}), \qquad A_\mu = (\phi, - \mathbf{A})
$$

where $\phi$ is the scalar potential and $\mathbf A$ is the spatial vector potential.

We know,

$$
F_{\mu \nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu
$$

Using this definition,

$$
F_{0i} = \partial_0 A_i - \partial_i A_0
$$

Since $A_0 = \phi$ and $A_i = - A_i^{\text{(spatial)}}$, we obtain the following

$$
F_{01} = - \partial_0 A_1 - \partial_1 A_0 = - \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial t} - \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} = E_x
$$

$$
F_{02} = - \partial_0 A_2 - \partial_2 A_0 = - \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial t} - \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} = E_y
$$

$$
F_{01} = - \partial_0 A_3 - \partial_3 A_0 = - \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial t} - \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial z} = E_z
$$

Therefore,

$$
F_{0i} = E_i
$$

The above equations can be written as 

$$
\mathbf{E} = - \nabla \phi - \frac{\partial \mathbf{A}}{\partial t}
$$

The above equations satisfy Maxwell's equation in following manner,

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = - \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} 
$$

Its $z$-component is

$$
\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial E_x}{\partial y} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}_z}{\partial t}
$$

Recall our potential expressions:

$$
E_x = - \partial_t A_x - \partial_x \phi
$$

$$
E_y = - \partial_t A_y - \partial_y \phi
$$

and

$$
B_z = \partial_x A_y - \partial_y A_x
$$

This equation will be shown to be true below. For now, we get

$$
\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial E_x}{\partial y} = - \partial_x \partial_t A_y - \partial_x \partial_y \phi + \partial_y \partial_t A_x + \partial_y \partial_x \phi
$$


$$
\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial E_x}{\partial y} = - \partial_x \partial_t A_y + \partial_y \partial_t A_x = - \partial_t (\partial_x A_y - \partial_y A_x)
$$

$$
\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial E_x}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial B_z}{\partial t}
$$

Now consider only spatial indices $i, j$ instead of $\mu, \nu$

$$
F_{i j} = \partial_i A_j - \partial_j A_i
$$

Because the spatial components of the lowered potential are negative, we have

$$
F_{i j} = - \partial_i A_j^{\text{(spatial)}} + \partial_j A_i^{\text{(spatial)}}
$$

For example,

$$
F_{12} = - \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y}
$$

The $z$-component of the magnetic field is

$$
B_z = \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y}
$$

This is true because 

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0
$$

according to Maxwell's equation. Also in vector calculus: the divergence of any sufficiently smooth curl is zero,

$$
\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{A}) = 0
$$

Therefore defining $\mathbf{B} = \nabla \times \mathbf{A}$ automatically satisfies Gauss's law for magnetism.

Comparing the two expressions,

$$
F_{12} = - B_z
$$

Similarly,

$$
F_{13} = B_y, \qquad F_{21} = B_z, \qquad F_{32} = B_x
$$

We can summarize all the spatial components as

$$
F_{ij} = - \epsilon_{ijk} B_k
$$

So the spatial part of the field-strength tensor encodes the magnetic field.

We choose the electromagnetic convention

$$
\mathbf{E} = - \nabla \phi - \frac{\partial \mathbf{A}}{\partial t}, \qquad
\mathbf{B} = \nabla \times \mathbf{A}
$$

With these conventions, the components of $F_{\mu\nu}$ are

$$
F_{0i} = E_i, \qquad
F_{ij} = - \epsilon_{ijk} B_k
$$

where $i,j,k\in\{1,2,3\}$ and $\epsilon_{ijk}$ is the three-dimensional Levi-Civita symbol.

The full tensor is

$$
F_{\mu \nu} = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & E_x & E_y & E_z \\
-E_x & 0 & -B_z & B_y \\
-E_y & B_z & 0 & -B_x \\
-E_z & -B_y & B_x & 0 \\
\end{pmatrix}
$$

The electric and magnetic fields are not two unrelated additions to the theory. They are different components of one spacetime tensor.

#### Maxwell's equations from the field strength

We can now see how gauge theory becomes a physical theory. Maxwell's equations arise in two complementary ways:

1. From the definition of $F_{\mu\nu}$, we obtain the homogeneous Maxwell equations.
2. From a gauge-invariant action, we derive the inhomogeneous Maxwell equations.

**The homogeneous equations: a geometric identity**

$$
F_{\mu \nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu 
$$

its derivatives satisfy the identity

$$
\partial_\lambda F_{\mu \nu} + \partial_\mu F_{\nu \lambda} + \partial_\nu F_{\lambda \mu} = 0
$$

This identity is also called the Bianchi identity.

In three-dimensional vector notation, it gives

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0
$$

and

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{E} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} = 0
$$

These are the two homogeneous Maxwell equations.



**The inhomogeneous equations: dynamics from an action**

To determine how the electromagnetic field responds to electric charge and current, we need an equation of motion.
Choose the electromagnetic Lagrangian density

$$
\boxed{\mathcal L_{\mathrm{EM}}=-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}}
$$

The factor $-1/4$ is conventional for our metric signature and normalization. The factor $1/4$ compensates for the double counting of antisymmetric index pairs.

To include an external four-current $j^\nu$, take

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{EM}}=-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} - j^\mu A_\mu
$$

We now vary the action with respect to $A_\nu$.

$$
\delta F_{\mu \nu} = \partial_\mu \delta A_\nu - \partial_\nu \delta A_\mu 
$$

$$
\delta \mathcal L_{\mathrm{EM}} = - \frac{1}{2} F^{\mu \nu} \delta F_{\mu \nu}
$$

Using antisymmetry, the two contributions combine:

$$
\delta \mathcal L_{\mathrm{EM}} = - F^{\mu \nu} \partial_\mu \delta A_\nu
$$

Including the source term,

$$
\delta S = \int d^4x \space [-F^{\mu \nu} \partial_\mu \delta A_\nu - j^\nu \delta A_\nu]
$$

Integrate the first term by parts and discard the boundary term, assuming the variations vanish appropriately at the boundary:

$$
\delta S = \int d^4x \space [\partial_\mu F^{\mu \nu} - j^\nu] \delta A_\nu
$$

The variation $\delta A_\nu$ is arbitrary. Stationarity of the action therefore requires

$$
\partial_\mu F^{\mu \nu} = j^\nu
$$

This is the covariant form of the inhomogeneous Maxwell equations in our conventions.

In three-dimensional notation, it gives

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \rho
$$

and

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{B} - \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} = \mathbf{j}
$$

We have now recovered all four Maxwell equations.
