**Action**

$$
S[x] = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} L(x, \dot{x}, t) dt
$$

The principle of stationary action says that the physical trajectory satisfies

$$
\delta S = 0
$$

Stationary does not necessarily mean minimum. The action can be locally minimized, maximized, or stationary in a more general sense.

Applying this principle gives the Euler–Lagrange equation:

$$
\frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{x}} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = 0
$$

Let's verify that it reproduces Newton's law.

For $L = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{x}^2 - V(x)$,

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial{\dot{x}}} = m \dot{x}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = - \frac{dV}{dx}
$$

Substitution gives

$$
m \ddot{x} + \frac{dV}{dx} = 0
$$

Since $F = - dV/dx$, this is exactly $m \ddot{x} = F$.

So the Lagrangian formulation is not a different physical law here. It is a systematic way to obtain the equations of motion.

**Why use a Lagrangian instead of Newton's law?**

The Lagrangian formulation becomes especially powerful when:

- A system has many interacting degrees of freedom.
- Constraints make forces difficult to calculate.
- Symmetries and conservation laws matter.
- We need a formulation compatible with special relativity and quantum field theory.

In field theory, the basic objects are fields spread throughout spacetime, rather than a finite collection of particle coordinates.

That leads us to the Lagrangian density.

**From particles to fields**

A field assigns a value to every point in spacetime. 

For example, temperature in a room can be represented by a scalar field $T(t,\mathbf{x})$. Each point has one temperature value.

n relativistic field theory, a field can be:

- A scalar, such as $\psi(x)$
- A vector, such as the electromagnetic potential $A_\mu(x)$
- A spinor, such as the electron field $\Psi(x)$

The symbol $x$ here represents the spacetime position $x^\mu = (t, \mathbf{x})$.

Our first example is a complex scalar field

$$
\psi(x) = \psi_1(x) + i \psi_2(x)
$$

It contains two real fields, $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$, packaged into one complex field.

Its complex conjugate is

$$
\psi^*(x) = \psi_1(x) - i \psi_2(x)
$$

We use a scalar field because its transformation properties are simpler than those of a spinor. It is a useful model for learning gauge theory, although it does not describe an electron.

**What is a Lagrangian density?**

For a particle, the Lagrangian $L$ is integrated over time.

For a field, the Lagrangian density $\mathcal L$ is integrated over both space and time:

$$
S = \int d^4x \space \mathcal{L}
$$

The Lagrangian density is the central object we construct. Once we know it, the action and equations of motion follow from it.

**What does the field Lagrangian contain?**

Your scalar-field Lagrangian is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{matter}} = \partial_\mu \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi - m^2 \psi^* \psi
$$

There are two terms.

**The kinetic term**

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{kin}} = \partial_\mu \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi
$$

A derivative measures how the field changes from one spacetime point to another. The kinetic term describes the field's spacetime variation.

With our metric convention,

$$
\partial_\mu \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi = \partial_t \psi^* \partial_t \psi - \nabla \psi^* \cdot \nabla \psi
$$

So it contains both time variation and spatial variation.

**The mass term**

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{mass}} = - m^2 \psi^* \psi
$$

Because $\psi^* \psi = |\psi|^2$, this term depends on the field's magnitude, not its phase. The parameter $m$ sets the mass scale appearing in the field equation.

The Lagrangian density is not simply a sum of positive energy densities. It contains relative signs chosen so that the resulting field equations have the correct relativistic form.

**Derive the scalar field equation**

In particle mechanics, the Euler–Lagrange equation differentiates with respect to $x$ and $\dot{x}$. 

$$
\frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{x}} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = 0
$$

In field theory, the corresponding equation is

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \psi} - \partial_\mu (\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \psi)}) = 0
$$

For

$$
\mathcal{L} = \partial_\mu \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi - m^2 \psi^* \psi
$$

first calculate

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \psi} = - m^2 \psi^*, \qquad \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \psi)} = \partial^\mu \psi^*
$$

The Euler–Lagrange equation for $\psi$ therefore becomes

$$
-m^2 \psi^* - \partial_\mu \partial^\mu \psi^* = 0
$$

Equivalently,

$$
(\Box + m^2) \psi^* = 0, \qquad \Box \equiv \partial_\mu \partial^\mu
$$

Varying with respect to $\psi^*$ gives the equation for $\psi$:

$$
(\Box + m^2) \psi = 0
$$

This is the Klein–Gordon equation, the relativistic field equation for a free scalar field.

**Global $U(1)$ symmetry and Noether's theorem**

A symmetry is a transformation that leaves a specified physical or mathematical structure unchanged.

For our scalar field, consider changing its complex phase:

$$
\psi(x) \longrightarrow \psi'(x) = e^{iq\alpha} \psi(x)
$$

This transformation rotates the field in the complex plane without changing its magnitude.

The set of these phase rotations forms the group $U(1)$.

**Why is the Lagrangian invariant?**

$$
\mathcal{L} = \partial_\mu \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi - m^2 \psi^* \psi
$$

Under the transformation,

$$
\psi' = e^{iq\alpha} \psi, \qquad \psi'^* = e^{-iq \alpha} \psi^*
$$

For the moment, suppose $\alpha$ is constant throughout spacetime.

Because differentiation does not act on a constant,

$$
\partial_\mu \psi' = e^{iq \alpha} \partial_\mu \psi
$$

Similarly,

$$
\partial_\mu \psi'^* = e^{-iq \alpha} \partial_\mu \psi^*
$$

Now multiply the transformed derivatives:

$$
\partial_\mu \psi'^* \partial^\mu \psi' = e^{-iq \alpha} e^{iq \alpha}  \partial_\mu \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi = \partial_\mu \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi
$$

The phase factors cancel.

The same happens for the mass term:

$$
\psi'^* \psi' = e^{-iq \alpha} e^{iq \alpha} \psi^* \psi = \psi^* \psi
$$

Therefore,

$$
\mathcal{L}' = \mathcal{L} 
$$

This is a global $U(1)$ symmetry: every spacetime point undergoes the same phase rotation.

**What changes if the phase depends on spacetime?**

$$
\psi'(x) = e^{iq \alpha(x)} \psi(x)
$$

This is a local transformation because the phase rotation can differ from one spacetime point to another.

$$
\partial_\mu \psi'(x) = e^{iq \alpha(x)} [\partial_\mu \psi + iq (\partial_\mu \alpha) \psi]
$$

There is an additional term,

$$
iq (\partial_\mu \alpha) \psi
$$

It appears because the phase itself now varies with position.

Consequently, the ordinary kinetic term is no longer invariant. The extra derivatives of $\alpha(x)$ prevent the cancellation we obtained for a constant phase.

This is the central problem that gauge theory solves.

**Noether's theorem: why symmetry produces a current**

Noether's theorem connects continuous global symmetries to conserved currents, provided the fields obey their equations of motion.

For an infinitesimal phase rotation,

$$
\delta \psi = iq \alpha \psi, \qquad \delta \psi^* = -iq \alpha \psi^*
$$

The corresponding current is

$$
j^\mu = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \psi)} \frac{\delta \psi}{\alpha} + \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \psi^*)} \frac{\delta \psi^*}{\alpha}
$$

Using the derivatives of the Lagrangian,

$$
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\psi)}
=\partial^\mu\psi^*,
\qquad
\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\psi^*)}
=\partial^\mu\psi,
$$

we obtain

$$
\boxed{j^\mu=iq(\psi\partial^\mu\psi^*-\psi^*\partial^\mu\psi)}
$$

This is the free scalar field's Noether current under our chosen transformation convention.

**Why is it conserved?**

$$
\partial_\mu j^\mu = iq(\psi \Box \psi^* - \psi^* \Box \psi)
$$

The terms involving products of first derivatives cancel because multiplication of the field components commutes.

From the Klein–Gordon equations,

$$
\Box \psi = - m^2 \psi, \qquad \Box \psi^* = - m^2 \psi^*
$$

so,

$$
\partial_\mu j^\mu = iq [- m^2 \psi \psi^* + m^2 \psi^* \psi] = 0
$$

This is the local continuity equation.

Writing $j^\mu=(\rho,\mathbf j)$, in our metric convention it becomes

$$
\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{j} = 0
$$

The equation expresses local conservation: a decrease of charge within a region must be accounted for by flux through its boundary.

$$
Q = \int d^3x \space j^0
$$

If the current vanishes sufficiently rapidly at spatial infinity, the total charge satisfies $dQ/dt=0$.

**Derive the gauge transformation of $A_\mu$**

We require the covariant derivative to obey

$$
D'_\mu\psi'=e^{iq\alpha(x)}D_\mu\psi.
$$

We know that

$$
\partial_\mu \psi' = e^{iq \alpha(x)} [\partial_\mu \psi + iq (\partial_\mu \alpha) \psi]
$$

The transformed covariant derivative is

$$
D'_\mu \psi' = (\partial_\mu + iq A'_\mu)\psi'
$$

Substituting the transformed matter field,

$$
D'_\mu\psi' = e^{iq\alpha} \left[\partial_\mu\psi + iq(\partial_\mu \alpha) \psi + iqA'_\mu \psi \right]
$$

But the required right-hand side is

$$
e^{iq\alpha}D_\mu\psi = e^{iq\alpha} \left(\partial_\mu\psi + iq A_\mu \psi \right).
$$

For these expressions to agree, the terms in brackets must agree. Assuming $q \ne 0$, we obtain

$$
\partial_\mu \alpha + A'_\mu = A_\mu
$$

Therefore,

$$
A'_\mu = A_\mu - \partial_\mu \alpha
$$

This is the gauge transformation law for the electromagnetic potential.

**Construct the invariant kinetic term**

$$
D'_\mu\psi' = e^{iq\alpha} D_\mu \psi 
$$

$$
(D'_\mu \psi')^* = e^{-iq\alpha} (D_\mu \psi)^*
$$

Multiplying them gives

$$
(D'_\mu\psi')^* (D'^\mu \psi') =  (D_\mu \psi)^* (D^\mu \psi)
$$

The phases cancel exactly.

Therefore, replacing ordinary derivatives with covariant derivatives gives a locally gauge-invariant matter Lagrangian:

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{matter} = (D_\mu \psi)^* (D^\mu \psi) - m^2 \psi^* \psi
$$

This replacement is called minimal coupling.

**Give the gauge field its own dynamics**

So far, $A_\mu$ appears in the matter Lagrangian, but we have not specified its own dynamics.

For electromagnetism, define the field-strength tensor

$$
F_{\mu \nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu
$$

Under a gauge transformation,

$$
F'_{\mu \nu} = \partial_\mu (A_\nu - \partial_\nu \alpha) - \partial_\nu (A_\mu - \partial_\mu \alpha) = F_{\mu \nu}
$$

Thus $F_{\mu\nu}$ is gauge invariant.

We use the electromagnetic Lagrangian density

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{gauge} = - \frac{1}{4} F_{\mu \nu} F^{\mu \nu}
$$

It is a local Lorentz scalar, is gauge invariant, is quadratic in the field strength, and gives Maxwell's equations when varied with respect to $A_\mu$.

The factor $1/4$ compensates for the antisymmetry of $F_{\mu\nu}$ and gives the standard normalization.

Combining the terms gives

$$
\mathcal{L} = (D_\mu \psi)^* (D^\mu \psi) - m^2 \psi^* \psi - \frac{1}{4} F_{\mu \nu} F^{\mu \nu}
$$

This describes a complex scalar field interacting with a dynamical $U(1)$ gauge field.

**Expand the covariant derivative: where the interaction comes from**

Start with

$$
D_\mu \psi = \partial_\mu \psi + iq A_\mu \psi
$$

$$
(D_\mu \psi)^* = \partial_\mu \psi^* - iq A_\mu \psi^*
$$

Now multiply them:

$$
(D_\mu \psi)^* (D^\mu \psi) = (\partial_\mu \psi^* - iq A_\mu \psi^*) \times (\partial^\mu \psi + iq A^\mu \psi)
$$

$$
= \partial_\mu \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi + iq A_\mu \psi \partial^\mu \psi^* - iq A_\mu \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi + q^2 A_\mu A^\mu \psi^* \psi
$$

$$
= \partial_\mu \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi + iq A_\mu (\psi \partial^\mu \psi^* - \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi) + q^2 A_\mu A^\mu |\psi|^2
$$

Recall that the free matter Noether current was

$$
j^\mu=iq(\psi\partial^\mu\psi^*-\psi^*\partial^\mu\psi)
$$

Therefore,

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{free}} + A_\mu j^\mu + q^2 A_\mu A^\mu |\psi|^2 - \frac{1}{4} F_{\mu \nu} F^{\mu \nu}
$$

We have discovered two types of matter–gauge-field interaction.

**The linear interaction**

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{int,linear}} = A_\mu j^\mu
$$

This term couples the electromagnetic potential to the free matter current.

It is linear in $A_\mu$, meaning that it contains one factor of the potential.

**The quadratic, or seagull, interaction**

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{int,quadratic}} = q^2 A_\mu A^\mu |\psi|^2
$$

This term contains two factors of the electromagnetic potential and two factors of the scalar field, one of which is complex conjugated.

It is often called the seagull term in scalar electrodynamics.

**Distinguish the free current from the full current**

The free matter current is

$$
j^\mu = iq(\psi \partial^\mu \psi^* - \psi^* \partial^\mu \psi)
$$

However, the current obtained by differentiating the full matter Lagrangian with respect to the gauge potential is

$$
J^\mu = iq [\psi (D^\mu \psi)^* - \psi^* D^\mu \psi]
$$

Expand the covariant derivatives:

$$
J^\mu = iq [\psi (\partial^\mu \psi^* - iq A^\mu \psi^*) - \psi^*(\partial^\mu \psi + iq A^\mu \psi)] \\
= j^\mu + 2 q^2 A^\mu |\psi|^2
$$

Therefore,

$$
J^\mu = j^\mu + 2 q^2 A^\mu |\psi|^2
$$

The full current includes the gauge-field-dependent contribution associated with the quadratic interaction.




**Derive Maxwell's equation from the action**

We now have a Lagrangian containing both matter and electromagnetism. To obtain the electromagnetic field equation, vary the action with respect to $A_\nu$.

For the gauge-field term,

$$
\mathcal{L}_\text{gauge} = - \frac{1}{4} F_{\mu \nu} F^{\mu \nu}
$$

variation and integration by parts give

$$
\delta S_\text{gauge} = \int d^4x \space (\partial_\mu F^{\mu \nu}) \delta A_\nu
$$

assuming the variation vanishes at the boundary.

For the minimally coupled scalar matter term,

$$
\delta S_\text{matter} = \int d^4x J^\nu \delta A_\nu
$$

Combining them,

$$
\delta S = \int d^4x \space (\partial_\mu F^{\mu \nu} + J^\nu) \delta A_\nu
$$

The variation $\delta A_\nu$ is arbitrary. Stationary action therefore requires,

$$
\partial_\mu F^{\mu \nu} = - J^\nu
$$

Sign-convention note:

$$
D_\mu = \partial_\mu + iq A_\mu, \qquad F_{\mu \nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu
$$

This is the sourced electromagnetic field equation. The electromagnetic field responds to the matter current, while the matter field evolves in the electromagnetic potential.

That is the central idea of a coupled field theory: the equations of motion of the two fields depend on one another.


**From a scalar field to an electron: the Dirac Lagrangian**

Our scalar-field theory illustrates gauge invariance, but an electron is not a scalar particle.

An electron has spin $1/2$. Its relativistic field is a Dirac spinor, denoted by $\Psi(x)$.

**What is a Dirac spinor?**

A Dirac spinor has four complex components in four-dimensional spacetime. These components transform according to the spinor representation of the Lorentz group.

The Dirac adjoint is defined as

$$
\bar{\Psi} = \Psi^\dagger \gamma^0
$$
