### Maxwell's equations

In the Newman-Penrose formalism, the antisymmetric Maxwell tensor, $F_{ij}$, is replaced by the three complex scalars.

$$
\phi_0 = F_{13} = F_{ij} l^i m^j,\\
\phi_1 = \frac{1}{2}(F_{12} + F_{43}) = \frac{1}{2} F_{ij}(l^i n^j + \bar{m}^i m^j), \\
\phi_2 = F_{42} = F_{ij} \bar{m}^i n^j
$$

and Maxwell's equations,

$$
F_{[ij;k]} = 0, \qquad g^{ik} F_{ij;k} = 0
$$

expressed in terms of  tetrad components and intrinsic derivatives, namely,

$$
F_{[ab|c]} = 0, \qquad \eta^{nm} F_{an|m} = 0
$$

are replaced by the equations,

$$
\phi_{1|1} - \phi_{0|4} = 0, \qquad \phi_{2|1} - \phi_{1|4} = 0, \\
\phi_{1|3} - \phi_{0|2} = 0, \qquad \phi_{2|3} - \phi_{1|2} = 0
$$

The explicit forms of these equations are readily found. Thus,

$$
\phi_{1|1} = \frac{1}{2} [F_{12,1} - \eta^{nm}(\gamma_{n11} F_{m2} + \gamma_{n21}F_{1m}) \\
+ F_{43,1} - \eta^{nm} (\gamma_{n41} F_{m3} + \gamma_{n31}F_{4m})] \\
= \phi_{1,1} - (\gamma_{131}F_{42} + \gamma_{241}F_{13}) \\
= D \phi_1 + \kappa \phi_2 - \pi \phi_0
$$

and similarly,

$$
\phi_{0|4} = \delta^* \phi_0 - 2 \alpha \phi_0 + 2 \rho \phi_1
$$

The first of Maxwell's equations, therefore, take the form

$$
D \phi_1 - \delta^* \phi_0 = (\pi - 2 \alpha) \phi_0 + 2 \rho \phi_1 - \kappa \phi_2
$$

And the remaining equations are

$$
D \phi_2 - \delta^* \phi_1 = - \lambda \phi_0 + 2 \pi \phi_1 + (\rho - 2 \epsilon) \phi_2
$$

$$
\delta \phi_1 - \Delta \phi_0 = (\mu - 2\lambda) \phi_0 + 2 \tau \phi_1 - \sigma \phi_2
$$

$$
\delta \phi_2 - \Delta \phi_1 = - \nu \phi_0 + 2 \mu \phi_1 + (\tau - 2 \beta) \phi_2
$$

Turning next to the energy-momentum tensor of the Maxwell field, we have

$$
T_{ab} = \eta^{cd} F_{ac} F_{bd} - \frac{1}{4} \eta_{ab} F_{ef} F^{ef}
$$

and in terms of the Maxwell scalars $\phi_0$, $\phi_1$, and $\phi_2$, we readily find that

$$
- \frac{1}{2} T_{11} = \phi_0 \phi_0^*; \qquad -\frac{1}{2} T_{13} = \phi_0 \phi_1^*;
$$

$$
- \frac{1}{4} (T_{13} + T_{34}) = \phi_1 \phi_1^*; \qquad -\frac{1}{2} T_{23} = \phi_1 \phi_2^*;
$$

$$
- \frac{1}{2} T_{22} = \phi_2 \phi_2^*; \qquad -\frac{1}{2} T_{33} = \phi_0 \phi_2^*;
$$

and the trace of $T_{ab}$ is, of course, zero. In accordance with equations 

$$
\Phi_{00} = \frac{1}{2} R_{11}; \space \Phi_{22} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{22}; \space \Phi_{02} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{33}; \space \Phi_{20} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{44}; \\
$$

$$
\Phi_{11} = - \frac{1}{4}(R_{12} + R_{34}); \space \Phi_{01} = - \frac{1}{2}R_{13}; \space \Phi_{10} = - \frac{1}{2}R_{14};
$$

$$
\Alpha = \frac{1}{24} R = \frac{1}{12}(R_{12} - R_{34}); \space \Phi_{12} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{23}; \space \Phi_{21} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{24}
$$

and

$$
R_{ij} = - \frac{8 \pi G}{c^4} (T_{ij} - \frac{1}{2} g_{ij} T)
$$

we can, therefore

$$
\text{set } \Lambda = 0 \text{ and replace } \Phi_{mn} \text{ by } \phi_m \phi_n^*
$$

apart from the constant proportionality, $-8 \pi G / c^4$.

In the Bianchi identities listed in equations before this from $(a-h)$ with Weyl and Ricci terms simplify considerably when use is made of Maxwell's equation. Thus, considering the terms in the derivatives of the Ricci scalars in equation $a$ of the Bianchi identity, we have

**Notes**

In electromagnetism, the electric and magnetic fields are combined into one antisymmetric rank-2 tensor.

$$
F_{ab} = - F_{ba}
$$

This is the electromagnetic field tensor. In ordinary coordinates, its six independent components correspond to the three components of $\mathbf E$ and three components of $\mathbf B$.

$$
F_{ab} \to (\mathbf{E}, \mathbf{B})
$$

$$
F_{ab} = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & - \mathbf{E}_x & - \mathbf{E}_y & - \mathbf{E}_z \\
\mathbf{E}_x & 0 & \mathbf{B}_z & - \mathbf{B}_y \\
\mathbf{E}_y & - \mathbf{B}_z & 0 & \mathbf{B}_x \\
\mathbf{E}_z & \mathbf{B}_y & - \mathbf{B}_x & 0 \\
\end{pmatrix}
$$

A general $4 \times 4$ tensor has 16 components, but antisymmetry gives

$$
\frac{4(4-1)}{2} = 6
$$

independent components.

The electric and magnetic fields are not independent under Lorentz transformations.

An observer moving relative to another observer can see part of what one observer calls an electric field as a magnetic field.

So instead of treating $\mathbf{E}, \mathbf{B}$ separately, relativity combines them into $F_{ab}$. This makes Maxwell's theory naturally covariant.

The four ordinary Maxwell equations can be written as two tensor equations.

The first is

$$
F_{[ab;c]} = 0
$$

or equivalently

$$
\nabla_{[a}F_{bc]} = 0
$$

This contains

$$
\nabla_c F_{ab} + \nabla_b F_{ca} + \nabla_a F_{bc} = 0
$$

It represents the homogeneous Maxwell equations:

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0
$$

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{E} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} = 0
$$

The second equation is

$$
\nabla^a F_{ab} = 0
$$

in vaccum. This contains the other two Maxwell equations:

$$
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = 0
$$

$$
\nabla \times \mathbf{B} - \frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} = 0
$$

when there are no charges or currents.

**Introduce Newman-Penrose tetrad**

Instead of using coordinate basis

$$
\partial_t, \partial_x, \partial_y, \partial_z
$$

the NP formalism uses the null tetrad

$$
(l^a, n^a, m^a, \bar{m}^a)
$$

Index convention

$$
1=l; \qquad 2=n; \qquad 3=m; \qquad 4=\bar{m};
$$

Therefore a tensor component such as

$$
F_{13}
$$

really means

$$
F_{13} = F_{ab} l^a m^b
$$

Similarly,

$$
F_{12} = F_{ab} l^a n^b
$$

$$
F_{43} = F_{ab} m^a \bar{m}^b
$$

Because $F_{ab}$ is real and antisymmetric. It has 6 real degrees of freedom. Three complex scalars also have 6 real degrees of freedom. So the electromagnetic field can be represented by three complex NP scalars:

$$
\phi_0, \qquad \phi_1, \qquad\phi_2
$$

They are defined as

$$
\phi_0 = F_{13} = F_{ab} l^a m^b
$$

$$
\phi_1 = \frac{1}{2} (F_{12} + F_{43}) = \frac{1}{2} F_{ab}(l^a n^b + \bar{m}^a m^b)
$$

$$
\phi_2 = F_{42} = F_{ab} \bar{m}^a n^b
$$

These are analogous to the Weyl scalars. 

$$
\Psi_0, \Psi_1, \cdots, \Psi_4
$$
except that the electromagnetic field has spin \(1\), so we only need three scalars.

**The energy momentum tensor**

The energy-momentum tensor $T_{ab}$ is a tensor that tells us how energy and momentum are distributed and flowing through spacetime. 

It is the relativistic generalization of several familiar quantities - mass density, energy density, momentum density, momentum flux, pressure, shear stresses. It is a $4 \times 4$ tensor. 

In relativity, energy and momentum are parts of same relativistic object. The four momentum is

$$
p^a = (E/c, \mathbf{p})
$$

So if we want a relativistically consistent description of matter, we need an object that describes not only where energy is, but also where momentum is and how both flow through spacetime.

That object is

$$
T^{ab}
$$

We use coordinates

$$
x^a = (ct, x, y, z)
$$

$$
T^{ab} = \begin{pmatrix}
T^{00} & T^{01} & T^{02} & T^{03} \\
T^{10} & T^{11} & T^{12} & T^{13} \\
T^{20} & T^{21} & T^{22} & T^{23} \\
T^{30} & T^{31} & T^{32} & T^{33} \\
\end{pmatrix}
$$ 

The first thing to understand is that different components have different physical meanings.

$T^{00}$ represents the energy density. It tells you tells you how much energy is contained per unit volume. $T^{0i}$ represents the flow of energy or, equivalently up to factors of $c$, the density of momentum. $T^{i0}$ represents momentum density. For ordinary matter with appropriate conventions, these are related. For example, if matter is moving in the $x$-direction, there will generally be a nonzero $T^{10}$.

$T^{ij}$ are the purely spatial components and describe momentum flowing through space. $T^{11}$ or $T^{22}$ represent pressure/stress in the $x$-direction or $y$-direction.  The off-diagonal terms, $T^{12}, T^{13}, T^{23}$ describe shear stresses.

The energy momentum tensor must transform into one another according to Lorentz transformations.

$$
T^{'ab} = \frac{\partial x^{'a}}{\partial x^c} \frac{\partial x^{'b}}{\partial x^d}  T^{cd}
$$

Electromagnetic fields carry energy and momentum. Therefore they curve spacetime. The electromagnetic stress-energy tensor is

$$
T_{ij} = F_{ik} F_{j}^k - \frac{1}{4} g_{ij} F_{kl} F^{kl}
$$

$$
T_{ab} = \eta^{cd} F_{ac} F_{bd} - \frac{1}{4} \eta_{ab} F_{ef} F^{ef}
$$

There are two parts. 

$$
F_{ik} F_{j}^k
$$

describes the energy momentum carried by the electromagnetic field.

$$
- \frac{1}{4} g_{ij} F_{kl} F^{kl}
$$

ensures that the tensor has correct covariant structure. 

The trace is zero

$$
T = g^{ij} T_{ij} = 0
$$

for the electromagnetic field in four spacetime dimensions. Therefore, the Einstein equation becomes

$$
R_{ij} = - \frac{8 \pi G}{c^4} (T_{ij} - \frac{1}{2}g_{ij}T)
$$

$$
R_{ij} = - \frac{8 \pi G}{c^4} T_{ij}
$$

So the Ricci tensor is directly proportional to the electromagnetic stress-energy tensor.

Expressing $T_{ab}$ using $\phi_0, \phi_1, \phi_2$. Instead of carrying around all the $F_{ab}$'s, we substitute

$$
F_{ab} \to \phi_0, \phi_1, \phi_2
$$

The source obtains

$$

$$
- \frac{1}{2} T_{22} = \phi_2 \phi_2^*; \qquad -\frac{1}{2} T_{33} = \phi_0 \phi_2^*;
