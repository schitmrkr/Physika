### Exterior Differentiation

Exterior differentiation is effected by an operator $d$ applied to forms. It converts p-forms to (p+1)-forms consistently with the following rules.

**a**. The operator $d$, applied to fnctions (or zero-forms) $f$, yeilds a one-form $df$ defined by 

$$
df(X) = \langle df, X \rangle = Xf
$$

for every $X \in T^1_0$. In particular, in a local coordinate basis

$$
df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^j} dx^j
$$

**b**. If $A_1$ and $A_2$ are two $p$-forms,

$$
d(\alpha A_1 + \beta A_2) = \alpha dA_1 + \beta d A_2
$$

**c**. If $A$ is a $p$-form and $B$ is a $q$-form,

$$
d(A \wedge B) = dA \wedge B + (-1)^p A \wedge dB
$$

**d**. Poincare's lemma, which requires that

$$
d(dA) = 0
$$

for every $p$-form $A$.

To clarify that the operator $d$ subject to the foregoing rules is well-defined, consided, first, the exterior derivative

$$
dA = d(A_{j_1..j_p} dx^{j_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{j_p})
$$

of a $p$-form $A$. By rules a, b, and d,

$$
dA = dA_{j_1..j_p} \wedge dx^{j_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{j_p}
$$

$$
= \frac{\partial A_{j_1..j_p}}{\partial x^k} dx^k \wedge dx^{j_1} \wedge .... \wedge dx^{j_p}
$$

If instead of the local coordinates ($x^j$) we had chosen a different set of local coordinates ($x^{j'}$)

$$
A_{j'_1 ... j'_p} = A_{j_1 ... j_p} \frac{\partial x^{j_1}}{\partial x^{j'_1}} ... \frac{\partial x^{j_p}}{\partial x^{j'_p}}
$$

and we should conclude that

$$
d(A_{j'_1...j'_p} dx^{j'_1} \wedge .. \wedge dx^{j'_p})
$$

$$
= d({A_{j_1..j_p}} \frac{\partial x^{j_1}}{\partial x^{j'_1}} ... \frac{\partial x^{j_p}}{\partial x^{j'_p}} {dx^{j'_1} \wedge .. \wedge dx^{j'_p}})
$$


$$
= \frac{\partial x^{j_1}}{\partial x^{j'_1}} ... \frac{\partial x^{j_p}}{\partial x^{j'_p}} dA_{j_1 .. j_p} \wedge dx^{j'_1} \wedge .. \wedge dx^{j'_p} \\
+ \frac{\partial^2 x^{j_1}}{\partial x^{k'} \partial x^{j'_2}} \frac{\partial x^{j_2}}{\partial x^{j'_2}} ... \frac{\partial x^{j_p}}{\partial x^{j'_p}} A_{j_1 .. j_p}  dx^k \wedge dx^{j'_1} \wedge .. \wedge dx^{j'_p} + .. \\
+ \frac{\partial x^{j_1}}{\partial x^{j'_1}} ... \frac{\partial x^{j_{p-1}}}{\partial x^{j'_{p-1}}} \frac{\partial^2 x^{j_p}}{\partial x^{k'} \partial x^{j'_p}} A_{j_1 .. j_p} dx^k \wedge dx^{j'_1} \wedge .. \wedge dx^{j'_p}
$$

All the terms on the right hand side of equation involving the second derivative of $x^{j_i}(i=1,..,p)$ vanish on account of their symmetry in $k'$ and $j'_1$ and the anti symmetry of the basis elements. Thus,

$$
dA_{j_1 .. j_p} \wedge dx^{j_1} \wedge .. \wedge dx^{j_p} = d(A_{j_1..j_p} dx^{j_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{j_p}) 
$$

Accordingly,

$$
d(A_{j'_1..j'_p} dx^{j'_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{j'_p}) = d(A_{j_1..j_p} dx^{j_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{j_p}) 
$$


Next, we verify rule **c** is consistent. By the rules **a**, **b**, and **d**. 

$$
d(A \wedge B) = d(A_{j_1...j_p} dx^{j_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{j_p} \wedge B_{k_1...k_q} dx^{k_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{k_q})
$$

$$
= \frac{\partial A_{j_1...j_p}}{\partial x^i} dx^i \wedge dx^{j_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{j_p} \wedge B_{k_1...k_q} dx^{k_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{k_q} + \\
A_{j_1...j_p} \frac{\partial B_{k_1...k_q}}{\partial x^i} dx^i \wedge dx^{j_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{j_p} \wedge dx^{k_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{k_q}
$$

$$
= dA \wedge B + \\
(-1)^p A_{J_1...j_p} dx^{j_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{j_p} \wedge (\frac{\partial B_{k_1...k_q}}{\partial x^i} dx^i \wedge dx^{k_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{k_q})
$$


$$
= dA \wedge B + (-1)^p A \wedge dB
$$

And finally to establish the consistency of rule **d**, we need only observe that

$$
d(dA) = d(\frac{\partial A_{j_1...j_p}}{\partial x^k} dx^k \wedge dx^{j_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{j_p})
$$

$$
= \frac{\partial^2 A_{j_1...j_p}}{\partial x^i \partial x^k} dx^i \wedge dx^k \wedge dx^{j_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{j_p} \equiv 0
$$

This completes tthe demonstration that the operation $d$ is, indeed well defined.

Notes:

Suppose $f(x,y,z)$ is a scalar field. The directional derivative is $Xf$

So if,

$$
X = X^x \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + X^y \frac{\partial}{\partial y} + X^z \frac{\partial}{\partial z}
$$

then,

$$
Xf = X^x \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + X^y \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} + X^z \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}
$$

We want to package all directional derivative into one object. Instead of asking seperately,

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}, \frac{\partial f}{\partial z}
$$

we want one object that can take any vector $X$ and tell us $Xf$. Define:

$$
df(X) = Xf
$$

This is the fundamental definition. $df$ takes a vector,

$$
df: T_p M \to \mathcal{R}
$$

Therefore, $df$ is a covector, or 1-form. 

Where does 

$$
df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^j} dx^j
$$

come from.

Remember that 

$$
(\frac{\partial}{\partial x^1}, \frac{\partial}{\partial x^2}, ..., \frac{\partial}{\partial x^n})
$$

is basis of the tangent space. It's dual basis is 

$$
(dx^1, dx^2, .., dx^n)
$$

The duality means:

$$
dx^i \frac{\partial}{\partial x^j} = \delta^i_J
$$

So any one-form can be written as:

$$
df = C_j dx^j
$$

Now evaluate it on

$$
X = X^j \frac{\partial}{\partial x^j}
$$

We get

$$
df(X) = C_j dx^j (X^j \frac{\partial}{\partial x^j})
$$

$$
df(X) = C_j X^j
$$

Thus,

$$
C_j = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x^j}
$$

Now we want $d$ to work on forms not just functions. So far,

$$
d: \Omega^0 \to \Omega^1
$$

We want,

$$
d: \Omega^p \to \Omega^{p+1}
$$

Remember what a p-form looks like

$$
A = A_{j_1..j_p} dx^{j_1} \wedge .. \wedge dx^{j_p}
$$

There are two pieces here:

a. the coefficient

$$
A_{j_1...j_p}
$$

These are ordinary function. Therefore,

$$
d A_{j_1...j_p} = \frac{\partial A_{j_1...j_p}}{\partial x^k} dx^k
$$

b. the basis form

$$
dx^{j_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{j_p}
$$

But

$$
dx^j = d(x^j)
$$

Therefore

$$
d(dx^j) = d(d(x^j)) = 0
$$

Thus, the natural definition is

$$
dA = dA_{j_1...j_p} \wedge dx^{j_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{j_p}
$$

$$
dA = \frac{\partial A_{j_1...j_p}}{\partial x^k} dx^k \wedge dx^{j_1} \wedge ... \wedge dx^{j_p}
$$

Example:

Differential of 1-form:

$$
A = A_x dx + A_y dy + A_z dz
$$

This is the one form.

Apply $d$

$$
dA = d(A_x dx) + d(A_y dy) + d(A_z dz)
$$

Since,

$$
d(dx) = d(dy) = d(dz) = 0
$$

we get,

$$
dA = dA_x \wedge dx + dA_y \wedge dy + dA_z \wedge dz
$$

Now,

$$
dA_x = \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x}dx + \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y}dy + \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial z}dz
$$

Therefore,

$$
dA_x \wedge dx = \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y}dy \wedge dx + \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial z}dz \wedge dx
$$

Doing same for others gives

$$
dA = (\frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y}) dx \wedge dy + ...
$$
