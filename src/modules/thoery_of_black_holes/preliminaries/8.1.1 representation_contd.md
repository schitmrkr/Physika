### The representation of the Weyl, the Ricci and the Riemann tensors (Ctnd.)

In the Newman-Penrose formalism, the ten independent components of the Weyl tensor are represented by the five complex scalars,

$$
\Psi_0 = - C_{1313} = - C_{pqrs} l^p m^q l^r m^s, \\
\Psi_1 = - C_{1213} = - C_{pqrs} l^p n^q l^r m^s, \\
\Psi_2 = - C_{1342} = - C_{pqrs} l^p m^q \bar{m}^r n^s, \\
\Psi_3 = - C_{1242} = - C_{pqrs} l^p n^q \bar{m}^r n^s, \\
\Psi_4 = - C_{2424} = - C_{pqrs} n^p \bar{m}^q n^r \bar{m}^s
$$

While it is clear on general grounds that the Weyl tensor is completely specified by the five complex scalars $\Psi_0 \cdots \Psi_4$, it will be convenient to have a general formula which expresses the different componenets of the Weyl tensor explicitly in terms of the five scalars. Such a formula can be obtained in following manner.

First we shall introduce a symbol which indicates the construction out of products of four quantities a linear combination of them which will have all the principle symmetries of the Riemann and the Weyl tensors. Thus, we shall let

$$
\{ w_p, x_q, y_r, z_s \} = w_p x_q y_r z_s - w_p x_q z_r y_s - x_p w_q y_r z_s + x_p w_q z_r y_s \\
+ y_p z_q w_r x_s - y_p z_q x_r w_s - z_p y_q w_r x_s + z_p y_q x_r w_s
$$

The linear combination so constructured is manifestly antisymmetric in $(p,q)$ and in $(r, s)$ and unchanged for the simultaneous interchange of the index pairs $(p, q)$ and $(r, s)$. In this notation, it is clear that the tensor components, $C_{pqrs}$ of the Weyl tensor myst have the representation

$$
C_{pqrs} = Q_{1212} \{ l_p n_q l_r n_s \} + Q_{3434} \{ m_p \bar{m}_q m_r \bar{m}_s \} + Q_{1234} \{ l_p n_q m_r \bar{m}_s \} \\
+ Q_{1314} \{ l_p m_q l_r \bar{m}_s \} + Q_{2324} \{ n_p m_q n_r \bar{m}_s \} \\
+ [ Q_{1313} \{ l_p m_q l_r m_s \} + Q_{2323} \{ n_p m_q n_r m_s \} + Q_{1213} \{ l_p n_q l_r m_s \} \\
+ Q_{1223} \{ l_p n_q n_r m_s \} + Q_{1323} \{ l_p m_q n_r m_s \} + Q_{1324} \{ l_p m_q n_r \bar{m}_s \}  \\
+ Q_{1334} \{ l_p m_q m_r \bar{m}_s \} + Q_{2334} \{ n_p m_q m_r \bar{m}_s \} + \text{complex conjugates} ] 
$$

Where $Q_{....}$ are coefficiets, unspecified for the present and 'complex conjugates' of the eight quantities in the square brackets on the right hand side are to be obtained by writting $\bar{m}$ wherever $m$ occursand index $4$ whereever $3$ occurs and vice versa.

The coefficients $Q_{....}$ in the representation above can be obtained by contracting both sides of the equation by appropriate products of the components of $l$, $n$, $m$, and $\bar{m}$. Thus, by contracting with $l^p m^q l^r m^s$, the left hand side, by definition, yeilds $- \Psi_0$, while the only surviving term on the right-hand side has the coefficient $Q_{2424}$; and, accordingly, $Q_{2424} = - \Psi_0$. Similarly, by contracting the equation with $l^p m^q l^r \bar{m}^s$, the only surviving term on the right hand-side has the coefficient $Q_{2423}$; and this coefficient must vansih since the left-hand side yeilds $C_{1314}$ which we have shown to be zero.  Proceeding in this fashion, making use of various definitions and requirements, we find

$$
Q_{1324} = \Psi_2; \space Q_{1212} = Q_{3434} = - (\Psi_2 + \Psi_2^*); \space Q_{1234} = (\Psi_2 - \Psi_2^*); \\

Q_{1313} = - \Psi_4; \space Q_{2424} = - \Psi_0; \space Q_{1213} = Q_{1334} = \Psi_3; \\

Q_{1224} = Q_{2443} = - \Psi_1; \space Q_{1314} = Q_{2324} = Q_{1323} = Q_{1424} = 0
$$

The required formula is, therefore,

$$
C_{pqrs} = - (\Psi_2 + \Psi_2^*) [\{ l_p n_q l_r n_s \} + \{ m_p \bar{m}_q m_r \bar{m}_s \}] + (\Psi_2 - \Psi_2^*) \{ l_p n_q m_r \bar{m}_s \} \\
+ [[ - \Psi_0 \{ n_p \bar{m}_q n_r \bar{m}_s \} - \Psi_4 \{ l_p m_q l_r m_s \} + \Psi_2 \{ l_p m_q n_r \bar{m}_s \} \\
- \Psi_1 [ \{ l_p n_q n_r \bar{m}_s \} + \{ n_p \bar{m}_q \bar{m}_r m_s \}] \\
+ \Psi_3 [\{ l_p n_q l_r m_s \} + \{ l_p m_q m_r \bar{m}_s \}] + \text{complex conjugate}
]]
$$

In particular,

$$
C_{1334} = \Psi_1; \space C_{2443} = \Psi_3; \space C_{1212} = C_{3434} = - (\Psi_2 + \Psi_2^*); \\
C_{1234} = (\Psi_2 - \Psi_2^*)
$$

Including the four components which vanish and allowing for complex conjugation, we have specified in equations anove all the distinct of the Weyl tensor.

And finally, the ten components of the Ricci tensor are defined in terms of the following four real and three complex scalars:

$$
\Phi_{00} = \frac{1}{2} R_{11}; \space \Phi_{22} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{22}; \space \Phi_{02} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{33}; \space \Phi_{20} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{44}; \\
$$

$$
\Phi_{11} = - \frac{1}{4}(R_{12} + R_{34}); \space \Phi_{01} = - \frac{1}{2}R_{13}; \space \Phi_{10} = - \frac{1}{2}R_{14};
$$

$$
\Alpha = \frac{1}{24} R = \frac{1}{12}(R_{12} - R_{34}); \space \Phi_{12} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{23}; \space \Phi_{21} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{24}
$$

**Notes**

This section takes the Weyl tensor with its $10$ independent real degrees of freedom and repackages them into five complex numbers:

$$
\Psi_0, \cdots , \Psi_4
$$

Then it does the analogous thing for the Ricci tensor, replacing its $10$ real components by four real $+$ three complex scalars, plus the scalar-curvature quantity $\Alpha$.

We already seperated 

$$
R_{abcd} = C_{abcd} + \text{Ricci part}
$$

where, $C_{abcd}$ is Weyl tensor. In four dimensions, Weyl tensor has $10$ independent components (Ricci part - $20$, $R_{abcd}$ - $10$).

The Newman–Penrose formalism now asks: Instead of carrying ten separate real Weyl components, can we package them more elegantly? 

We package them into $5$ complex numbers which has 10 real numbers in them.

$$
\Psi_0, \cdots , \Psi_4
$$

Recall Newman-Penrose tetrad,

$$
\boxed{e_{(1)} = l, \space e_{(2)} = n, \space e_{(3)} = m, \space e_{(4)} = \bar{m}}
$$

The vectors satisfy,

$$
l \cdot n = 1 \\
m \cdot \bar{m} = -1
$$

with all other scalar products to zero.

Thus,

$$
l^2 = n^2 = m^2 = \bar{m}^2 = 0
$$

The indices $1,2,3,4$ corresponds to

$$
1 \leftrightarrow l \\
2 \leftrightarrow n \\
3 \leftrightarrow m \\
4 \leftrightarrow \bar{m} \\
$$

Definition of the five Weyl scalars:

$$
\Psi_0 = - C_{1313} \\
\Psi_1 = - C_{1213} \\
\Psi_2 = - C_{1342} \\
\Psi_3 = - C_{1242} \\
\Psi_4 = - C_{2424} 
$$

Here, $\Psi_0 \cdots \Psi_5$ are simply particular contractions of the Weyl tensor with the null tetrad.
For example,

$$
\Psi_0 = - C_{1313} = - C_{pqrs} l^p m^q l^r m^s
$$

and so on for other scalars.

The Weyl tensor has 10 independent real components. But the Newman–Penrose basis is complex because $m$ and $\bar m$ are complex conjugates. Therefore it is natural to combine real components into complex quantities.

For example, $\Psi_0$ contains two real degrees of freedom:

$$
\Psi_0 = Re(\Psi_0) + i Im(\Psi_0)
$$

The same applies to the other four. So, $5$ complex implies $10$ real degrees of freedom.

Now we want to construct a tensor automatically having the same algebraic symmetries as the Riemann tensor. 

Recall the Riemann symmetries:

$$
R_{pqrs} = - R_{qprs} \\
R_{pqrs} = - R_{pqsr} \\
R_{pqrs} = R_{rspq}
$$

Instead of repeatedly writing combinations that enforce these properties, we define the curly-bracket notation


$$
\{ w_p, x_q, y_r, z_s \} = w_p x_q y_r z_s - w_p x_q z_r y_s \\ 
- x_p w_q y_r z_s + x_p w_q z_r y_s \\
+ y_p z_q w_r x_s - y_p z_q x_r w_s \\
- z_p y_q w_r x_s + z_p y_q x_r w_s
$$

This construction has a simple purpose. It makes $\{ w_p, x_q, y_r, z_s \}$ antisymmetric in first pair $(p,q)$ and second pair $(r,s)$, and makes it symmetric under exchange of pairs $(p,q) \leftrightarrow (r,s)$. 

Therefore, $\{ w_p, x_q, y_r, z_s \}$ already has basic Riemann symmetries.

Suppose we wrote

$$
l_p n_q l_r n_s
$$

This by itself does not have all the symmetries of the Riemann tensor.

But,

$$
\{ l_p, n_q, l_r, n_s \}
$$

does.

Therefore, instead of writing an enormous general expression with dozens of individual terms, we can write

$$
C_{pqrs} = Q_{1212} \{ l_p, n_q, l_r, n_s \} + \cdots
$$

and know automatically that the resulting tensor has the required pair symmetries.

As we know,

$$
C_{pqrs} = Q_{1212} \{ l_p n_q l_r n_s \} + Q_{3434} \{ m_p \bar{m}_q m_r \bar{m}_s \} + Q_{1234} \{ l_p n_q m_r \bar{m}_s \} \\
+ Q_{1314} \{ l_p m_q l_r \bar{m}_s \} + Q_{2324} \{ n_p m_q n_r \bar{m}_s \} \\
+ [ Q_{1313} \{ l_p m_q l_r m_s \} + Q_{2323} \{ n_p m_q n_r m_s \} + Q_{1213} \{ l_p n_q l_r m_s \} \\
+ Q_{1223} \{ l_p n_q n_r m_s \} + Q_{1323} \{ l_p m_q n_r m_s \} + Q_{1324} \{ l_p m_q n_r \bar{m}_s \}  \\
+ Q_{1334} \{ l_p m_q m_r \bar{m}_s \} + Q_{2334} \{ n_p m_q m_r \bar{m}_s \} + \text{complex conjugates} ] 
$$

Which combinations of \(1,2,3,4\) do we actually need? 

Think of $C_{pqrs}$ as a matrix of antisymmetric pairs. 

$$
C_{pqrs} = - C_{qprs}
$$

the first two indices behave like an antisymmetric pair:

$$
[pq]
$$

There are only six possible antisymmetric pairs:

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
4 \\
2
\end{pmatrix} = 6
$$

$$
\boxed{12, 13, 14, 23, 24, 34}
$$

Same idea goes for 

$$
[rs]
$$

Therefore, we can imagine $6 \times 6$ matrix

$$
C_{[pq][rs]}
$$

Since we know that

$$
C_{[pq][rs]} = C_{[rs][pq]}
$$

is symmetric, a $6 \times 6$ matrix has 

$$
6(7)/2 = 21
$$

independent entries. The displayed expression is not a list of 13 independent Weyl components. The $Q$'s are coefficients in an expansion, and they are constrained by:

- Riemann symmetries
- algebraic Bianchi identity
- Weyl trace-free condition
- reality/complex-conjugation conditions


The $Q_{....}$'s are initially unknown coefficients. We determine the coefficients by projecting $C_{pqrs}$ onto suitable basis vectors. We can write $C_{pqrs}$ is the linear combination of the "basis". The basis are these Riemann-symmetry-preserving curly-bracket tensors.

$$
C_{pqrs} = \sum Q \times \{ \text{tetrad vectors} \}
$$

Example: why $Q_{2424} = \Psi_0$

Consider:

$$
Q_{2424} \{n_p, \bar{m}_q, n_r, \bar{m}_s \}
$$

Contract with $l^p m^q l^r m^s$ - $1313$ because

$$
l^p n_p = 1, \space m^q \bar{m}_q = -1
$$

oing the complete curly-bracket contraction gives the appropriate surviving coefficient, leading to

$$
Q_{2424} = - \Psi_0
$$

Similarly, we can determine all $Q_{....}$'s by contracting with appropriate basis tensor.

The final Weyl can be reconstructed by

$$
C_{pqrs} = - (\Psi_2 + \Psi_2^*) [\{ l_p n_q l_r n_s \} + \{ m_p \bar{m}_q m_r \bar{m}_s \}] + (\Psi_2 - \Psi_2^*) \{ l_p n_q m_r \bar{m}_s \} \\
+ [[ - \Psi_0 \{ n_p \bar{m}_q n_r \bar{m}_s \} - \Psi_4 \{ l_p m_q l_r m_s \} + \Psi_2 \{ l_p m_q n_r \bar{m}_s \} \\
- \Psi_1 [ \{ l_p n_q n_r \bar{m}_s \} + \{ n_p \bar{m}_q \bar{m}_r m_s \}] \\
+ \Psi_3 [\{ l_p n_q l_r m_s \} + \{ l_p m_q m_r \bar{m}_s \}] + \text{complex conjugate}
]]
$$

Finally,

$$
\Phi_{00} = \frac{1}{2} R_{11}; \space \Phi_{22} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{22}; \space \Phi_{02} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{33}; \space \Phi_{20} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{44}; \\
$$

$$
\Phi_{11} = - \frac{1}{4}(R_{12} + R_{34}); \space \Phi_{01} = - \frac{1}{2}R_{13}; \space \Phi_{10} = - \frac{1}{2}R_{14};
$$

$$
\Alpha = \frac{1}{24} R = \frac{1}{12}(R_{12} - R_{34}); \space \Phi_{12} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{23}; \space \Phi_{21} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{24}
$$

Why these set of indices?

Because of the null tetrad structure and complex conjugation. In the null tetrad,

$$
e_1 = l, \space e_2 = n, \space e_3 = m, \space e_4 = \bar{m}, \space
$$

So the Ricci tensor has components

$$
R_{ab} = \begin{pmatrix}
R_{11} & R_{12} & R_{13} & R_{14} \\
R_{21} & R_{22} & R_{23} & R_{24} \\
R_{31} & R_{32} & R_{33} & R_{34} \\
R_{41} & R_{42} & R_{43} & R_{44} \\
\end{pmatrix}
$$

But the Ricci tensor is symmetric:

$$
\boxed{R_{ab} = R_{ba}}
$$

Therefore this becomes

$$
R_{ab} = \begin{pmatrix}
R_{11} & R_{12} & R_{13} & R_{14} \\
R_{12} & R_{22} & R_{23} & R_{24} \\
R_{13} & R_{23} & R_{33} & R_{34} \\
R_{14} & R_{24} & R_{34} & R_{44} \\
\end{pmatrix}
$$

So there are exactly ten independent entries:

$$
\boxed{11, 22, 33, 44, 12, 13, 14, 23, 24, 34}
$$

**Why they are paired the way they are?**

The key is that $m$ and $\bar{m}$ are complex conjugates. herefore the indices $3$ and $4$ are special. They are not two unrelated directions. They are complex conjugates:

$$
e_3 = m, \space e_4 = \bar{m}
$$

This means complex conjugation acts on tetrad indices as

$$
\boxed{1 = 1^*, \space 2 = 2^*, \space 3 = 4^*, \space 4 = 3^*, \space}
$$

This is the main reason for the particular pairings.

Let's look at $R_{13}$. We have

$$
R_{13} = R(l,m)
$$

Take the complex conjugate:

$$
R_{13}^* = R(l,m)^* = R(l, \bar{m}) = R_{14}
$$

Therefore, they are not two independent complex quantities. That is why

$$
\Phi_{01} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{13}
$$

and 

$$
\Phi_{10} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{14}
$$

Consequently,

$$
\Phi_{01} = \Phi_{10}^*
$$

So the pair $(13,14)$ comes directly from conjugation

$$
m \leftrightarrow \bar{m}
$$

Same thing happens for $R_{23}$ and $R_{24}$.

What about $R_{33}$ and $R_{44}$?

$$
R_{33} = R(m,m), \space R_{44} = R(\bar{m}, \bar{m})
$$

Thus,

$$
R_{33} = R_{44}^*
$$

So, we define,

$$
\Phi_{02} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{33}
$$

and

$$
\Phi_{20} = - \frac{1}{2} R_{44}
$$

Why is $R_{11}$ not paired with $R_{22}$?

Because they are each indepenently real.

$$
R_{11} = R_{11}^*, \space R_{22} = R_{22}^*
$$

Why are $R_{12}$ and $R_{34}$ not put together?

Because they are also each indepenently real.

$$
R_{12} = R_{12}^*
$$

and

$$
R_{34} = R_{43}^* = R_{34}^*
$$

Instead of keeping them separately, the NP formalism forms their sum and difference.

The sum is 

$$
\Phi_{11} = - \frac{1}{4} (R_{12} + R_{34})
$$

The difference is proportional to the scalar curvature:

$$
R = 2 (R_{12} - R_{34})
$$

Therefore,

$$
A = \frac{1}{24} R
$$

Therfore, finally we
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{Ricci components} & \text{Relation} & \text{NP scalar} \\ \hline
R_{11} & \text{real} & \Phi_{00} \\[1mm]
R_{22} & \text{real} & \Phi_{22} \\[1mm]
R_{12},\,R_{34} & \text{real pair} & \Phi_{11},\,\Alpha \\[1mm]
R_{13},\,R_{14} & R_{14}=R_{13}^{*} & \Phi_{01},\,\Phi_{10} \\[1mm]
R_{23},\,R_{24} & R_{24}=R_{23}^{*} & \Phi_{12},\,\Phi_{21} \\[1mm]
R_{33},\,R_{44} & R_{44}=R_{33}^{*} & \Phi_{02},\,\Phi_{20}
\end{array}
$$
