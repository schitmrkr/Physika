### Parallel displacements and geodesics

Let $Y$ represent a contravariant vector-field. Consider its variation along a curve $\lambda$ on $M$. The change $\delta Y$ in $Y$ caused by a displacement along $\lambda$ resulting from an increment $\delta t$ in $t$. 

$$
(\delta Y)^j = Y^j_{,k} \frac{dx^k(\lambda(t))}{dt} \delta t
$$

In Euclidean geometry and in a Cartesian system of coordinates, one would say that $Y$ is 'parallely propagated' along $\lambda$ if $\delta Y = 0$

In general differentiable manifold with a connection, one defines, analogously, that a vector $Y$ is parallely propagated along $\lambda$, if

$$
(DY)^j = (\nabla_{\partial_k} Y)^j \frac{dx^k(\lambda(t))}{dt} \delta t = Y^j_{;k} \frac{dx^k(\lambda(t))}{dt} \delta t = 0
$$

or, alternatively, if 

$$
(Y^j_{,k} + Y^l \Gamma^j_{lk}) \frac{dx^k(\lambda(t))}{dt} \delta t = 0
$$

In other words,

$$
(\delta Y)^j = - Y^l \Gamma^j_{lk} \frac{dx^k(\lambda(t))}{dt} \delta t
$$

In particular, for the tangent vector to the curve $\lambda$, $dx^j (\lambda(t))/dt$ parallely propagated along $\lambda$,

$$
\delta(\frac{dx^j(\lambda(t))}{dt}) = - \Gamma^j_{lk} \frac{dx^l(\lambda(t))}{dt} \frac{dx^k(\lambda(t))}{dt} \delta t
$$

A curve $\lambda$ on $M$ is said to be a geodesic if the tangent vector to $\lambda$, parallely propagated, remains a multiple of itself. This condition, for $\lambda$ to be a geodesic is clearly,

$$
\frac{dx^j(\lambda(t))}{dt} - \Gamma^j_{lk} \frac{dx^l(\lambda(t))}{dt} \frac{dx^k(\lambda(t))}{dt} \delta t \\
$$

$$
= [1-\phi(t) \delta t] (\frac{dx^j(\lambda(t))}{dt} + \frac{d^2 x^j(\lambda(t))}{dt^2} \delta t)
$$

where $\phi(t)$ is some function of $t$. In the limit $\delta t \to 0$, the equation of geodesic becomes

$$
\frac{d^2 x^j}{dt} + \Gamma^j_{lk} \frac{dx^l}{dt} \frac{dx^k}{dt} = \phi(t) \frac{dx^j}{dt}
$$

It can be verified that if we reparametrize the curve $\lambda$ by the variable 

$$
s = \int^t dt'' \exp{\int^{t''} dt' \phi(t')}
$$

the equation becomes 

$$
\frac{d^2 x^j}{ds} + \Gamma^j_{lk} \frac{dx^l}{ds} \frac{dx^k}{ds} = 0
$$

**Notes**

What does it mean for $Y$ to stay parallel as we move along a curve? 

This is not trivial on a curved manifold because

$$
Y(t) \in T_{\lambda(t)}M
$$

while

$$
Y(t+\delta t) \in T_{\lambda(t+\delta t)}M
$$

The $\nabla$ gives us a way to compare.

The curve is $\lambda(t)$. Its tangent/velocity vector is 

$$
\frac{d \lambda}{dt} = \frac{dx^k}{dt} \partial_k
$$

How does $Y$ change along $\lambda$?

We should take the covariant derivative in the direction of the curve's tangent vector.

$$
\nabla_{\dot{\lambda}} Y
$$

is the covariant rate of change of $Y$ along the curve.

Since,

$$
\dot{\lambda} = \frac{dx^k}{dt} \partial_k
$$

we have,

$$
\nabla_{\dot{\lambda}} Y = \nabla_{\frac{dx^k}{dt} \partial_k} Y =  \frac{dx^k}{dt} \nabla_{\partial_k} Y
$$

This is the central idea.

Suppose,

$$
Y = Y^j \partial_j
$$

If we temporarily ignore the fact that the basis itself can change, the ordinary change of the components along the curve would be

$$
\frac{dY^j}{dt}
$$

By the chain rule,

$$
\frac{dY^j}{dt} = \frac{\partial Y^j}{\partial x^k} \frac{dx^k}{dt}
$$

Using the notation,

$$
Y^j_{,k} = \partial_k Y^j
$$

we get

$$
\frac{dY^j}{dt} = Y^j_{,k} \frac{dx^k}{dt}
$$

For a small change $\delta t$,

$$
(\delta Y)^j = Y^j_{,k} \frac{dx^k}{dt} \delta t
$$

Why isn't this enough?

In ordinary Eucledian space, the cartesian basis vector $\partial_x, \partial_y, \partial_z$ do not change from point to point. In a general manifold, the basis can change. 

So the covariant change along the curve,

$$
(\nabla_{\partial_k} Y)^j = Y^j_{,k} + \Gamma^j_{lk}Y^l
$$

$$
\nabla_{\dot{\lambda}} Y = \frac{dx^k}{dt} \nabla_{\partial_k} Y
$$

$$
(\nabla_{\dot{\lambda}} Y)^j = (Y^j_{,k} + \Gamma^j_{lk}Y^l) \frac{dx^k}{dt} 
$$

for small interval $\delta t$,

$$
(D Y)^j = (Y^j_{,k} + \Gamma^j_{lk}Y^l) \frac{dx^k}{dt} \delta t 
$$

Now we define that $Y$ is **parallel transported** along $\lambda$ if its covariant change along the curve is 0.

$$
\nabla_{\dot{\lambda}} Y = 0
$$

$$
(Y^j_{,k} + \Gamma^j_{lk}Y^l) \frac{dx^k}{dt}  = 0
$$

$$
\frac{dY^j}{dt}  + \Gamma^j_{lk}Y^l \frac{dx^k}{dt} = 0
$$

$$
\frac{dY^j}{dt}  = - \Gamma^j_{lk}Y^l \frac{dx^k}{dt}
$$

This says something subtle. Parallel transport does not mean,

$$
\frac{dY^j}{dt} = 0
$$

**What if we try to parallel transport the tangent vector itself?**

Every curve has a tangent vector,

$$
\dot{\lambda} = \frac{dx^j}{dt} \partial_j
$$

Set $Y = \dot{\lambda}$

$$
Y^j = \frac{dx^j}{dt}
$$

$$
\frac{Y^j}{dt} = \frac{d^2x^j}{dt^2}
$$

So for parallel transport equation,

$$
\frac{dY^j}{dt}  + \Gamma^j_{lk}Y^l \frac{dx^k}{dt} = 0
$$

becomes,

$$
\frac{d^2x^j}{dt^2} + \Gamma^j_{kl} \frac{dx^l}{dt} \frac{dx^k}{dt} = 0
$$

This is the familiar geodesic equation. But there is a subtlety.
