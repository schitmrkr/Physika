### Spin

In classical mechanics, a rigid object has two kind of angular momentum: orbital $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}$ (associated with motion of center of mass)  and spin $\mathbf{S} = I \omega$ (motion about the center of mass). Like in classical mechanics, electron also carries angular momentum somewhat analogous to classical spin. 

The algebraic theory of spin is carbon copy of the theory of orbital angular momentum, beginning with the fundamental commutation relations:

$$
[S_x, S_y] = i \hbar S_z, \space [S_y, S_z] = i \hbar S_x, \space [S_z, S_x] = i \hbar S_y
$$

It follows as before that eigenvectors of $S^2$ and $S_z$ satisfy, 

$$
S^2 | s \space m \rangle = \hbar^2 s(s+1) | s \space m \rangle; \space S_z | s \space m \rangle = \hbar m | s \space m \rangle
$$

and

$$
S_\pm | s \space m \rangle = \hbar \sqrt{s(s+1) - m(m \pm 1)} | s \space (m \pm 1) \rangle
$$

where,

$$
s = 0, 1/2, 1, 3/2, ....; \space m = -s, -s+1, ..., s-1, s
$$


#### Spin 1/2

Spin 1/2  is the spin of particles that make up ordinary matter (protons, neutrons, electrons) as well as all quarks and leptons. For $s = 1/2$ there are only two eigenstate: $| \frac{1}{2} \frac{1}{2}\rangle$ - spin up and $| \frac{1}{2} (-\frac{1}{2}) \rangle$ -spin down. Using these as basis vectors, the general state of a spin $1/2$ particle can be represented by two element column matrix (or spinor):

$$
\chi = \begin{pmatrix}
a \\
b
\end{pmatrix} = a \chi_+ + b \chi_-
$$

with

$$
\chi_+ = \begin{pmatrix}
1 \\
0
\end{pmatrix}
$$

representing spin up,

$$
\chi_- = \begin{pmatrix}
0 \\
1
\end{pmatrix}
$$

representing spin down.

Now we apply the spin operator to basis vectors,

$$
S^2 \chi_+ = \frac{3}{4} \hbar^2 \chi_+, \space S^2 \chi_- = \frac{3}{4} \hbar^2 \chi_-,
$$

If we write $S^2$ as $2 \times 2$ matrix,

$$
S^2 = \begin{pmatrix}
c & d \\
e & f
\end{pmatrix}
$$

We get

$$
S^2 = \frac{3}{4} \hbar^2 \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 \\
0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Similary,

$$
S_z \chi_+ = \frac{\hbar}{2} \chi_+, \space S_z \chi_- = \frac{\hbar}{2} \chi_-
$$

from which follows

$$
S_z = \frac{\hbar}{2}  \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 \\
0 & -1
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Meanwhile,

$$
S_+ \chi_- = \hbar \chi_+, \space S_- \chi_+ = \hbar \chi_-, \space S_+ \chi_+ = S_- \chi_- = 0
$$

so,

$$
S_+ = \hbar \begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}, \space
S_- = \hbar \begin{pmatrix}
0 & 0 \\
1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Now $S_\pm = S_x \pm i S_y$, so $S_x = \frac{1}{2} (S_+ + S_-)$ and $S_y = \frac{1}{2} (S_+ - S_-)$, and hence

$$
S_x = \frac{\hbar}{2} \begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}, \space
S_y = \frac{\hbar}{2} \begin{pmatrix}
0 & -i \\
i & 0
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Since, $S_x$, $S_y$, and $S_z$ all carry a faactor of $\hbar/2$, it is tidier to write $S = (\hbar/2) \sigma$, where

$$
\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}, \space
\sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & -i \\
i & 0
\end{pmatrix}, \space
\sigma_z = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 \\
0 & -1
\end{pmatrix}, \space
$$

These are called Pauli spin matrices.
