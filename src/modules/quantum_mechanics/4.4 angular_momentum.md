### Angular Momentum

Hydrogen atams are labeled by three quantum number, $n$, $l$, and $m$. The principle quantum number $n$ determines the energy of the state; $l$ and $m$ are related to the orbital angular momentum. 

Clasically the angular momentum of a particle is given by

$$
\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{r} \times \mathbf{p}
$$

which is to say

$$
L_x = y p_z - z p_u, \space L_y = z p_x - x p_z, \space L_z = x p_y - y p_x
$$

The corresponding quantum operators are obtained by the standard prescription $p_x \to -i\hbar \partial/\partial x$, $p_y \to -i\hbar \partial/\partial y$ and $p_z \to -i\hbar \partial/\partial z$.

Now we hope to obtain the eigenvalues of the angular momentum operator.

#### Eigenvalues

The operators $L_x$ and $L_y$ do not commute; In fact

$$
[L_x, L_y] = [y p_z - z p_y, z p_x - x p_z] \\
= [y p_z, z p_x] - [y p_z, x p_z] - [z p_y, z p_x] + [z p_y, x p_z]
$$

From canonical commutation relations, we know that only operator that fail to commute are $x$ with $p_x$, $y$ with $p_y$, and $z$ with $p_z$. So middle terms drop out, leaving

$$
[L_x, L_y] = y p_x [p_z, z] + x p_y [z, p_z] = i \hbar (xp_y - yp_x) = i \hbar L_z
$$

Similarly,

$$
[L_x, L_y] = i \hbar L_z, \space [L_y, L_z] = i \hbar L_x, [L_z, L_x] = i \hbar L_y
$$

These are fundamental commutation relations for angular momentum; everything follows from them.

***
**Notes**

Angular momentum in 2D can be expressed as:

$$
L_z = x p_y - y p_x
$$

This can be generalized as components of 3D angular momentum vector as

$$
\mathbf{L} =\begin{bmatrix}
\hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\
x & y & z \\
p_x & p_y & p_z
\end{bmatrix}
$$

In the equation $[L_x, L_y]$, consider the first term

$$
[y p_z, z p_x] 
$$

We know that,

$$
[AB, C] = A[B, C] + [A, C]B
$$

So,

$$
[y p_z, z p_x] = y[p_z,z p_x] + [y, z p_x] p_z = y[p_z,z p_x] = y [p_z​, z]p_x ​+ y z[p_z​, p_x​] \\
= y[p_z​, z]p_x = -i \hbar y p_x
$$

Also the second and third,

$$
[y p_z, x p_z] = [z p_y, z p_x] = 0
$$

Now for the fourth term,

$$
[z p_y, x p_z] = x[z, p_z]p_y = i \hbar x p_y
$$

Therefore,

$$
[L_x, L_y] = i \hbar (xp_y - yp_x) = i \hbar L_z
$$

***

Notice that $L_x$, $L_y$ and $L_z$ are incompatible observables. According to generalized uncertainty principle

$$
\sigma^2_{L_x} \sigma^2_{L_y} \ge (\frac{1}{2i} \langle i \hbar L_z \rangle)^2 = \frac{\hbar^2}{4} \langle L_z \rangle^2
$$

or,

$$
\sigma_{L_x} \sigma_{L_y} \ge \frac{\hbar}{2} \left| \langle L_z \rangle \right|
$$

the square of total angular momentum

$$
L^2 \equiv L_x^2 + L_y^2 + L_z^2
$$

does commute with $L_x$,

$$
[L^2, L_x] = [L_x^2, L_x] + [L_y^2, L_x] + [L_z^2, L_x] \\
= 0 + L_y^2 L_x - L_x L_y^2 + L_z^2 L_x - L_x L_z^2 \\ 
= L_y L_y L_x - L_y L_x L_y + L_y L_x L_y - L_x L_y L_y +\\  L_z L_z L_x - L_z L_x L_z + L_z L_x L_z - L_x L_z L_z \\
= L_y[L_y, L_x] + [L_y, L_x]L_y + L_z[L_z, L_x] + [L_z, L_x]L_z \\
= L_y (-i\hbar L_z) + (-i\hbar L_z) L_y + L_z(i\hbar L_y) + (i\hbar Ly) L_z \\
= 0
$$

It follows:

$$
[L^2, L_x] = 0, \space [L^2, L_y] = 0, \space [L^2, L_z] = 0
$$

or 

$$
[L^2, \mathbf{L}] = 0
$$

So, $L^2$ is compatible with each component of $\mathbf{L}$, we can hope to get simultaneous eigenstates of $L^2$ and (say) $L_z$:

$$
L^2 f = \lambda f, \space L_z f = \mu f
$$

We'll use ladder operator technique,

$$
L_{\pm} \equiv L_x \pm i L_y
$$

Its commutator with $L_z$ is

$$
[L_z, L_{\pm}] = [L_z, L_x] \pm i [L_z, L_y] = i\hbar L_y \pm i(-i\hbar L_x) = \pm \hbar (L_x \pm i L_y)
$$

so,

$$
[L_z, L_{\pm}]  = \pm \hbar L_\pm
$$

Also,

$$
[L^2, L_\pm] = 0
$$

Claim: if $f$ is eigen function of $L^2$ and $L_z$, then so is $L_\pm f$. The equation above says

$$
L^2 (L_\pm f) = L_\pm (L^2 f) = L_\pm(\lambda f) = \lambda (L_\pm f)
$$

So, $L_\pm f$ is an eigenfunction of $L^2$ with the same eigenvalue $\lambda$, 

$$
L_z (L_\pm f) = (L_z L_\pm - L_\pm L_z)f + L_\pm L_z f = \pm \hbar L_\pm + L_\pm (\mu f)\\
L_z (L_\pm f) = (\mu \pm \hbar) (L_\pm f)
$$

So, $L_\pm f$ is an eigenfunction of $L_z$ with the new eigenvalue $\mu \pm \hbar$. We call $L_+$ the raising operator, because it increases the the eigenvalue of $L_z$ by $\hbar$, and $L_-$ the lowering operater decreses the eigenvalue of $L_z$ by $\hbar$. 

***

**Notes**

Incompatibility means that we cannot prepare a quantum state in which both components have perfectly definite values simultaneously.


$$
\sigma^2_{A} \sigma^2_{B} \ge \frac{1}{2} | \langle [A, B] \rangle |
$$

Hence, $[L^2, L_x] = 0$ means that $L^2$ and $L_x$ are compatible. Meaning we can determine both simultaneously.

Eigenfunction:

An Eigenfunction of an operator is a function, that when the operator acts on it, it doesn't change its basic form but only get multiplied by a number.

For an operator $\hat{A}$

$$
\hat{A} f = a f
$$

Here, $\hat{A}$ = operator, $f$ = eigenfunction, $a$ = eigenvalue. Example:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}
$$

Take

$$
\frac{d}{dx} e^{kx} = k e^{kx}
$$

So, $e^{kx}$ is an eigenfunction of the derivative operator, with eigenvalue $k$.

***

For a given value of $\lambda$, we obtain a ladder of states, with each "rung" seperated by $\hbar$ in the eigenvalue of $L_z$. To ascend we use the raising operator and vice versa. But this process cannot go on forever. Eventually we are going to reach a state for which the $z$ component exceeds total and that cannot be. There must exist a "top rung" $f_t$ such that

$$
L_+ f_t = 0
$$

Let $\hbar l$ be eigenvalue of $L_z$ at the top rung

$$
L_z f_t = \hbar l f_t; \space L^2 f_t = \lambda f_t
$$

Now,

$$
L_\pm L_\mp = (L_x \pm iL_y)(L_x \mp iL_y)\\
= L_x^2 + L_y^2 \mp i(L_x L_y - L_y L_x) \\ 
= L^2 - L_z^2 \mp i (i\hbar L_z)
$$

or

$$
L^2 = L_\pm L_\mp + L_z^2 \mp \hbar L_z
$$

It follows that,

$$
L^2 f_t = (L_- L_+ + L_z^2 + \hbar L_z) f_t \\
= (0 + \hbar^2 l^2 + \hbar^2 l) f_t = \hbar^2 l(l+1) f_t
$$

Hence,

$$
\lambda = \hbar^2 l(l+1)
$$

Meanwhile there is also a bottom rung, $f_b$, such that

$$
L_- f_b = 0
$$

Simlarly, we get

$$
L^2 f_b = \hbar^2 \bar{l} (\bar{l} - 1) f_b
$$

Therfore,

$$
\lambda = \hbar^2 \bar{l} (\bar{l} - 1)
$$

So either $\bar{l} = l + 1$ or else $\bar{l} = - l$.

So the eigen values of $L_z$ are $m\hbar$, where $m$ goes from $-l$ to $l$ in $N$ integer steps. In particular it follows that $l = -l+N$, hence $l = N/2$, so $l$ must be an integer of half integer. The eighenfunctions are characterized by the number $l$ and $m$,

$$
L^2 f^m_l = \hbar^2 l(l+1) f^m_l
$$

$$
L_z f^m_l = \hbar m f^m_l
$$

where,

$$
l = 0, 1/2, 1, 3/2, ..; \space m = -l, -l+1, ...., l-1, l.
$$
