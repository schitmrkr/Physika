### Lagrangian Density

$$
\mathcal{L} = - \frac{1}{2} \partial^\mu \phi \partial_\mu \phi - \frac{1}{2} m^2 \phi^2 + \Omega_0
$$

Let $\phi(x)$ be a real scalar field in spacetime, where $x^\mu=(t,\mathbf{x})$ in units $c=\hbar=1$.

Because $\phi$ is a Lorentz scalar,

$$
\phi'(x') = \phi(x)
$$

We want a Lorentz-invariant Lagrangian density $\mathcal L$ that describes a free field with mass $m$.

The Lagrangian density must be a Lorentz scalar. The simplest terms involving $\phi$ and its first derivatives are

$$
\phi^2, \qquad \partial^\mu \phi \partial_\mu \phi,  \qquad 1
$$

The derivative term is a Lorentz scalar because its spacetime indices are contracted. Therefore, the most general Lagrangian density using these terms is

$$
\mathcal{L} = A \partial^\mu \phi \partial_\mu \phi + B \phi^2 + C
$$

For a scalar field, the Euler–Lagrange equation is

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \phi} - \partial_\mu (\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \phi)}) = 0
$$

First, differentiate with respect to the field:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \phi} = 2 B \phi
$$

Next, differentiate with respect to its derivative:

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \phi)} = 2 A \partial^\mu \phi
$$

Substituting these expressions into the Euler–Lagrange equation gives

$$
2 B \phi - 2 A \partial^\mu \phi = 0
$$

Dividing by $-2A$, we obtain

$$
(\Box - \frac{B}{A}) \phi = 0
$$

where, $\Box = \partial^\mu \partial_\mu$.

**Require the Klein–Gordon equation**

For a free scalar field of mass $m$, using the metric signature $(-,+,+,+)$, the Klein–Gordon equation is

$$
(\Box-m^2)\phi=0
$$

Comparing it with the equation derived above, we find

$$
\frac{B}{A} = m^2 \implies B = A m^2 
$$

Our Lagrangian becomes

$$
\mathcal{L} = A (\partial^\mu \phi \partial_\mu \phi + m^2 \phi^2) + C
$$

The equation of motion fixes the ratio between the coefficients, but not their overall normalization.

For the conventional normalization of a real scalar field, choose

$$
A = - \frac{1}{2}, \qquad B = - \frac{1}{2} m^2, \qquad C = \Omega_0
$$

Thus,

$$
\mathcal{L} = - \frac{1}{2} \partial^\mu \phi \partial_\mu \phi - \frac{1}{2} m^2 \phi^2 + \Omega_0
$$

This gives the Lagrangian density.

**Verify the result**

Substituting the proposed Lagrangian into the Euler–Lagrange equation,

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \phi} = - m^2 \phi
$$

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \phi)} = - \partial^\mu \phi
$$

Thus,

$$
- m^2 \phi - \partial_\mu \partial^\mu \phi = 0
$$

which gives

$$
(\Box - m^2) \phi = 0
$$

The proposed Lagrangian therefore reproduces the Klein–Gordon equation, as required.

### Euler Lagrange Equation

For a scalar field, the Euler–Lagrange equation is

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \phi} - \partial_\mu (\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \phi)}) = 0
$$

**Define the action**

In classical field theory, the action is the integral of the Lagrangian density over spacetime:

$$
S[\phi] = \int d^4x \space \mathcal{L}(\phi, \partial_\mu \phi, x^\mu)
$$

Here, $\phi$ is the scalar field, $\partial_\mu \phi$ is spacetime derivative, $\mathcal{L}$ is the Lagrangian density and $d^4x = dt \space d^3x$ is the spacetime volume element.

Unlike ordinary mechanics, where the Lagrangian depends on a coordinate $q(t)$ and its time derivative $\dot q(t)$, a field Lagrangian depends on the field and its derivatives with respect to all four spacetime coordinates.

Consider a small variation of the field:

$$
\phi(x) \longrightarrow \phi(x) + \delta \phi(x)
$$

The variation $\delta\phi$ is an arbitrary small change in the field configuration, subject to the condition that it vanishes at the spacetime boundary.

The action changes by $\delta S$. The principle of stationary action requires

$$
\delta S = 0
$$

To calculate this variation, we first determine how the Lagrangian density changes.

Since $\mathcal L$ depends on both $\phi$ and $\partial_\mu\phi$, the multivariable chain rule gives

$$
\delta \mathcal{L} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \phi} \delta \phi + \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \phi)} \delta(\partial_\mu \phi)
$$

The variation and spacetime derivative commute, so

$$
\delta(\partial_\mu \phi) = \partial_\mu (\delta \phi)
$$

Therefore,

$$
\delta \mathcal{L} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \phi} \delta \phi + \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \phi)} \partial_\mu (\delta \phi)
$$

Because $S=\int d^4x \, \mathcal L$, we obtain

$$
\delta S = \int d^4x \, [\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \phi} \delta \phi + \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \phi)} \partial_\mu (\delta \phi)]
$$

The difficulty is that the second term contains a derivative of $\delta\phi$. We want to express the entire integral in terms of $\delta\phi$ itself.

**Apply integration by parts**

Define, for convenience,

$$
A^\mu = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \phi)}
$$

Then the second term in the action variation is

$$
\int d^4x \, A^\mu \partial_\mu (\delta \phi)
$$

Using the product rule,

$$
\partial_\mu (A^\mu \delta \phi) = (\partial_\mu A^\mu) \delta \phi + A^\mu \partial_\mu (\delta \phi)
$$

$$
A^\mu \partial_\mu (\delta \phi) = \partial_\mu (A^\mu \delta \phi) - (\partial_\mu A^\mu) \delta \phi 
$$

Integrating both sides over spacetime gives

$$
\int d^4x \, A^\mu \partial_\mu (\delta \phi) = \int d^4x \, \partial_\mu (A^\mu \delta \phi) - \int d^4x \, (\partial_\mu A^\mu) \delta \phi 
$$

he first integral is a boundary term. Since $\delta\phi=0$ on the boundary, that term vanishes. Consequently,

$$
\int d^4x \, A^\mu \partial_\mu (\delta \phi) = - \int d^4x \, (\partial_\mu A^\mu) \delta \phi 
$$

Substituting back the definition of $A^\mu$,

$$
\int d^4x \, \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \phi)} \partial_\mu (\delta \phi) = - \int d^4x \, \partial_\mu (\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \phi)}) \delta \phi 
$$

Substitute the integration-by-parts result into $\delta S$,

$$
\delta S = \int d^4x \, [\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \phi}  - \partial_\mu (\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \phi)})]\delta \phi
$$

The principle of stationary action says that $\delta S=0$, so

$$
\int d^4x \, [\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \phi}  - \partial_\mu (\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \phi)})]\delta \phi = 0
$$

Thus,

$$
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \phi}  - \partial_\mu (\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\mu \phi)}) = 0
$$

This is the Euler–Lagrange equation for a scalar field.
