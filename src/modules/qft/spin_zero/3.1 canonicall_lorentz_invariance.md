**Is this theory Lorentz invariant?**

$$
H = \int d^3p \sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4} \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}) \tilde{a}(\mathbf{p})
$$

$$
E_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i=0}^n \sqrt{\mathbf{p}_i^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4}
$$

We will start with the classical physics of a real scalar field $\phi(x)$. For Lorentz invariance,

$$
\phi(x) = \bar{\phi}(\bar{x})
$$

This then implies that the equation of motion for $\phi(x)$ must be the same as that for $\bar{\phi}(\bar{x})$. We have already met an equation of this type: the Klein-Gordon equation,

$$
(\partial^2 + m^2) \phi(x) = 0, \space c=1
$$

We adopt this equation as equation of motion we would like $\phi(x)$ to obey. It should be emphasized at this point that we are doing classical physics of a real scalar field. We are not to think of $\phi(x)$ as a quantum wave function. Thus, there should not be any factors of $\hbar$ in this version of the Klein-Gordon equation. This means that the parameter $m$ must have dimensions of inverse length; $m$ is not (yet) to be thought of as a mass.

**Action**

The equation of motion can be derived from variation of an action $S = \int dt \space L$, where $L$ is the lagrangian. Since the Klein Gordon equation is local, we expect that the lagrangian can be written as the space integral of
a lagrangian density

$$
\mathcal{L}: L = \int d^3x \space \mathcal{L}
$$

Thus, 

$$
S = \int d^4x \space \mathcal{L}
$$

The integration measure $d^4x$ is Lorentz invariant. 

$$
\bar{x}^\mu = \Lambda^\mu_\nu x^\nu
$$

$$
d^4\bar{x} = |\text{det} \Lambda| d^4x = d^4x
$$

Thus, for action to be Lorentz invariant, the lagrangian density must be a Lorentz scalar

$$
\mathcal{L}(x) = \bar{\mathcal{L}}(\bar{x})
$$

Then we have,

$$
\bar{S} = \int d^4\bar{x} \space \bar{\mathcal{L}}(\bar{x}) = \int d^4x \space \mathcal{L}(x) = S
$$

Any simple function of $\phi$ is a Lorentz scalar, and so are products of derivatives with all indices contracted, such as $\partial^\mu \phi \partial_\mu \phi$. We will take for $\mathcal{L}$,

$$
\mathcal{L} = - \frac{1}{2} \partial^\mu \phi \partial_\mu \phi - \frac{1}{2} m^2 \phi^2 + \Omega_0
$$

where $\Omega_0$ is an arbitrary constant. 

We find the equation of motion (also known as Euler-Lagrange equation) by making infinitesimal variation $\delta \phi(x)$ in $\phi(x)$ and requiring the corresponding variation of the action to vanish

$$
0 = \delta S \\
= \int d^4x \space [- \frac{1}{2} \partial^\mu \delta \phi \space \partial_\mu \phi - \frac{1}{2} \partial^\mu \phi \space \partial_\mu \delta \phi - m^2 \phi \space \delta \phi] \\ 
= \int d^4x [+ \partial^\mu \partial_\mu \phi - m^2 \phi] \space \delta \phi
$$

Since $\delta \phi$ has an arbitrary $x$ dependence, the above equation can be true if and only if

$$
(- \partial^2 + m^2) \phi = 0
$$

One solution of Klein Gordon equation is a plane wave of form $\exp(i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} \pm i \omega t)$, where $\mathbf{k}$ is an arbitrary real wave-vector, and

$$
\omega = + (\mathbf{k}^2 + m^2)^{1/2}
$$

The general solution (assuming boundary conditions that require $\phi$ to remain finite at spatial infinity) is then

$$
\phi(\mathbf{x}, t) = \int \frac{d^3k}{f(k)} [a(\mathbf{k}) e^{i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - i \omega t} + b(\mathbf{k}) e^{i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} + i \omega t}]
$$

where $a(\mathbf{k})$ and $b(\mathbf{k})$ are arbitrary functions of the wave vector $\mathbf k$, and $f(k)$ is a redundant function of the magnitude of $\mathbf k$ which we have inserted for later convenience.

We are trying to interpret eq. above as a real classical field, but this formula does not generically result in $\phi$ being real. We must impose $\phi^*(x) = \phi(x)$, where

$$
\phi^*(\mathbf{x}, t) = \int \frac{d^3k}{f(k)} [a^*(\mathbf{k}) e^{-i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} + i \omega t} + b^*(\mathbf{k}) e^{- i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - i \omega t}] \\
= \int \frac{d^3k}{f(k)} [a^*(\mathbf{k}) e^{-i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} + i \omega t} + b^*(-\mathbf{k}) e^{+ i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - i \omega t}]
$$

We see that $\phi^*(x) = \phi(x)$ requires 

$$
b^*(-\mathbf{k}) = a(\mathbf{k})
$$

Imposing this condition, we can rewrite $\phi$ as

$$
\phi(\mathbf{x}, t) = \int \frac{d^3k}{f(k)} [a(\mathbf{k}) e^{i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - i \omega t} + a^*(-\mathbf{k}) e^{i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} + i \omega t}] \\
= \int \frac{d^3k}{f(k)} [a(\mathbf{k}) e^{i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - i \omega t} + a^*(\mathbf{k}) e^{-i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} + i \omega t}] \\
= \int \frac{d^3k}{f(k)} [a(\mathbf{k}) e^{i kx} + a^*(\mathbf{k}) e^{-i kx}]
$$

where $kx = \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - \omega t$ is the Lorentz-invariant product of the four-vectors $x^\mu = (t, \mathbf{x})$ and $k^\mu = (\omega, \mathbf{k})$: $kx = k^\mu x_\mu = g_{\mu \nu} k^\mu x^\nu$. Note that

$$
k^2 = k^\mu k_\mu = \mathbf{k}^2 - \omega^2 = - m^2
$$

A four-momentum $k^\mu$ that obeys $k^2 = -m^2$ is said to be on mass shell. It is now convenient to choose $f(k)$ such that $d^3k/f(k)$ is lorentz invariant. An integration measure that is manifestly invariant under orthochronous
Lorentz transformations is

$$
d^4k \space \delta(k^2 + m^2) \space \theta(k^0)
$$

where, $\delta(x)$ is the Dirac delta function, $\theta(x)$ is a unit step function, and $k^0$ is treated as an independent integration variable. We then have

$$
\int_{-\infin}^{+\infin} dk^0 \space \delta(k^2 + m^2) \space \theta(k^0) = \frac{1}{2 \omega}
$$

Here, we have used the rule

$$
\int_{-\infin}^{+\infin} dx \space \delta(g(x)) = \sum_i \frac{1}{|g'(x_i)|}
$$

where $g(x)$ is any smooth function of $x$ with simple zeros at $x = x_i$ in our case, the only zero is at $k^0 = \omega$.

Thus we see that if we take $f(k) \propto \omega$, then $d^3k/f(k)$ will be Lorentz invariant. We will take $f(k) = (2\pi)^3 2\omega$. It is then convenient to give the corresponding Lorentz-invariant differential its own name:

$$
\tilde{dk} \equiv \frac{d^3k}{(2 \pi)^3 2 \omega}
$$

Thus we finally have

$$
\phi(x) = \int \tilde{dk} [a(\mathbf{k})e^{ikx} + a^*(\mathbf{k})e^{-ikx}]
$$

We also invert the formula to get $a(\mathbf{k})$ in terms of $\phi(x)$. We have

$$
\int d^3x \space e^{-ikx} \space \phi(x) = \frac{1}{2 \omega} a(\mathbf{k}) + \frac{1}{2 \omega} e^{2 i \omega t} a^*(-\mathbf{k})
$$

$$
\int d^3x \space e^{-ikx} \space \partial_0 \phi(x) = - \frac{i}{2} a(\mathbf{k}) + \frac{i}{2} e^{2 i \omega t} a^*(-\mathbf{k})
$$

We can combine these to get

$$
a(\mathbf{k}) = \int d^3x \space e^{-ikx} [i \partial_0 \phi(x) + \omega \phi(x)] \\
= i \int d^3x \space e^{-ikx} \space \overleftrightarrow{\partial_0} \phi(x)
$$

where $f \overleftrightarrow{\partial_\mu} g \equiv f(\partial_\mu g) - (\partial_\mu f) g$, and $\partial_0 \phi = \partial \phi/ \partial t = \dot{\phi}$. Note that $a(\mathbf{k})$ is time independent. 


Now that we have the lagrangian, we can construct the hamiltonian by the usual rules. Recall that, given a lagrangian $L(q_i, \dot{q}_i)$ as a function of some coordinates $q_i$ and their time derivatives $\dot{q}_i$, the conjugate momenta are given by $p_i = \partial L /\partial \dot{q}_i$, and the hamiltonian by $H = \sum p_i \dot{q}_i - L$. 

In our case, the role of $q_i(t)$ is played by $\phi (x,t)$, with $x$ playing the role of a (continuous) index. The appropriate generalizations are then

$$
\Pi(x) = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{\phi}(x)}
$$

and

$$
\mathcal{H} = \Pi \dot{\phi} - \mathcal{L}
$$

where $H$ is the hamiltonian density, and the hamiltonian itself is $H = \int d^3x \space \mathcal{H}$. In our case, we have

$$
\Pi(x) = \dot{\phi}(x)
$$

and

$$
\mathcal{H} = \frac{1}{2} \Pi + \frac{1}{2}(\nabla \phi)^2 + \frac{1}{2} m^2 \phi^2 - \Omega_0
$$

Using $\phi(x) = \int \tilde{dk} [a(\mathbf{k})e^{ikx} + a^*(\mathbf{k})e^{-ikx}]$, we can write $H$ in terms of $a(\mathbf{k})$ and $a^*(\mathbf{k})$ coefficients:

$$
H = - \Omega_0 V + \frac{1}{2} \int \tilde{dk} \tilde{dk}' d^3x \space [ \\
(- i \omega a(\mathbf{k})e^{ikx} + i \omega a^*(\mathbf{k})e^{-ikx})(- i \omega' a(\mathbf{k}')e^{ik'x} + i \omega' a^*(\mathbf{k}')e^{-ik'x}) \\
+ (+ i \mathbf{k} a(\mathbf{k})e^{ikx} - i \mathbf{k} a^*(\mathbf{k})e^{-ikx}) \cdot (+ i \mathbf{k}' a(\mathbf{k}')e^{ik'x} - i \mathbf{k}' a^*(\mathbf{k}')e^{-ik'x}) \\
+ m^2 (a(\mathbf{k})e^{ikx} + a^*(\mathbf{k})e^{-ikx}) (a(\mathbf{k}')e^{ik'x} + a^*(\mathbf{k}')e^{-ik'x}) 
]
$$

$$
= - \Omega_0 V + \frac{1}{2} \int \tilde{dk} \omega (a^*(\mathbf{k}) a(\mathbf{k}) + a(\mathbf{k}) a^*(\mathbf{k}))
$$


Let us take up the quantum theory now. We can go from classical to quantum mechanics via canonical quantization. This means that we promote $q_i$ and $p_i$ to operators, with commutation relations $[q_i ,q_j] = 0$, $[p_i, p_j] = 0$, and $[q_i, p_j] = i \hbar \delta_{ij}$. In the Heisenberg picture, these operators should be taken at equal times. In our case, where the “index” is continuous(and we have set $\hbar = 1$), we have

$$
[\phi(\mathbf{x},t), \phi(\mathbf{x}',t)] = 0 \\
[\Pi(\mathbf{x},t), \Pi(\mathbf{x}',t)] = 0 \\
[\phi(\mathbf{x},t), \Pi(\mathbf{x}',t)] = i \delta^3 (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}')
$$

From these canonical commutation relations, we can deduce

$$
[a(\mathbf{k}), a(\mathbf{k}')] = 0 \\
[a^\dagger(\mathbf{k}), a^\dagger(\mathbf{k}')] = 0 \\
[a(\mathbf{k}), a^\dagger(\mathbf{k}')] = (2 \pi)^3 2 \omega \space \delta^3(\mathbf{k} - \mathbf{k}') \\
$$

We can now rewrite the hamiltonian as

$$
H = \int \tilde{dk} \space \omega a^\dagger(\mathbf{k}) a(\mathbf{k}) + (\mathcal{E}_0 - \Omega_0)V
$$

where,

$$
\mathcal{E}_0 = \frac{1}{2} (2 \pi)^{-3} \int d^3k \space \omega
$$

is the total zero-point energy of all the oscillators per unit volume.

If we integrate only up to a maximum value of $\Lambda$, a number known as the ultraviolet cutoff, we find

$$
\mathcal{E}_0 = \frac{\Lambda^4}{16 \pi^2}
$$

where, $\Lambda \gg m$.

**Notes**

In classical mechanics, we can describe the motion of a system using its Lagrangian $L$. The action is

$$
S = \int dt \space L
$$

For a field, there is a value of the field at every point in space. So instead of one ordinary Lagrangian, we use a Lagrangian density $\mathcal L$, defined at each spacetime point.

$$
L = \int d^3x \space \mathcal{L}
$$

Then the action is

$$
S = \int d^4x \space \mathcal{L}
$$

Think of $\mathcal{L}$ as the local contribution to the action. Integrating it over all spacetime gives the total action.

The spacetime coordinates transform according to

$$
\bar{x}^\mu = \Lambda^\mu_\nu x^\nu
$$

Under this transformation, the integration measure becomes

$$
d^4\bar{x} = |\text{det} \Lambda| d^4x = d^4x
$$

If we want the action to be invariant, we choose the Lagrangian density to transform as a scalar:

$$
\mathcal{L}(x) = \bar{\mathcal{L}}(\bar{x})
$$

$$
\bar{S} = \int d^4\bar{x} \space \bar{\mathcal{L}}(\bar{x}) = \int d^4x \space \mathcal{L}(x) = S
$$

Construct the Lagrangian density

$$
\mathcal{L} = - \frac{1}{2} \partial^\mu \phi \partial_\mu \phi - \frac{1}{2} m^2 \phi^2 + \Omega_0
$$

First, $\phi$ is a scalar, so $\phi^2$ is also a scalar. 
Second, $\partial_\mu \phi$ transforms as a four-vector. Contracting its indices gives a scalar:

$$
\partial^\mu \phi \partial_\mu \phi
$$

Every term in $\mathcal L$ is therefore a scalar. This makes the action Lorentz invariant.

**Deriving the Klein Gordon**

We slightly change the field

$$
\phi(x) \to \phi(x) + \delta \phi(x)
$$

We require the action to be stationary under arbitrary small variations that vanish at the boundary:

$$
\delta S = 0
$$

This is the principle of stationary action.

Our Lagrangian density is

$$
\mathcal{L} = - \frac{1}{2} \partial^\mu \phi \partial_\mu \phi - \frac{1}{2} m^2 \phi^2 + \Omega_0
$$

From this density, and setting $\delta S = 0$, we can obtain Klein Gordon equation

$$
(- \partial^2 + m^2) \phi = 0
$$

**Expressing the field as plane waves**

The Klein–Gordon equation is a linear differential equation. One way to solve it is to try a plane wave:

$$
\phi(x) = e^{(i \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - i \omega t)}
$$

Here, $\mathbf k$ is a wave vector describing spatial oscillations, and $\omega$ is the angular frequency, describing temporal oscillations.

For the metric signature $(-,+,+,+)$, we define

$$
k^\mu = (\omega, \mathbf{k}), \qquad kx = \mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - \omega t
$$

Thus the plane wave can be written as $e^{ikx}$.

When we substitute it into the Klein–Gordon equation, we obtain the dispersion relation

$$
\omega^2 = \mathbf{k}^2 + m^2
$$

Taking the positive-frequency solution gives

$$
\omega = \sqrt{\mathbf{k}^2 + m^2}
$$

This is the relativistic energy-momentum relation in natural units, with $\omega$ playing the role of the energy.

The Klein–Gordon equation is linear, so sums of its solutions are also solutions.

Therefore, we can construct a general solution by adding plane waves with different momenta:

$$
\phi(\mathbf{x}, t) = \int \frac{d^3k}{f(k)} [\phi(\mathbf x,t)=\int\frac{d^3k}{f(k)}\left[a(\mathbf k)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x-i\omega t}+b(\mathbf k)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x+i\omega t}\right]
$$

Here, $a(\mathbf k)$ and $b(\mathbf k)$ are coefficients specifying how much of each plane wave is present. The function $f(k)$ is a normalization factor that we are free to choose for convenience.

Think of this as the Fourier decomposition of a complicated wave: rather than describing the entire field at once, we describe it as a superposition of simple waves.

**Why must the coefficients satisfy a condition?**

We want a real scalar field, meaning

$$
\phi^*(x) = \phi(x)
$$

Taking the complex conjugate reverses the signs in the exponential:

$$
e^{i\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} - i \omega t} \longrightarrow e^{- i\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{x} + i \omega t} 
$$

Now change the integration variable $\mathbf k \to - \mathbf k$ in the appropriate term. Comparing the coefficients of matching plane waves gives

$$
b^*(-\mathbf{k}) = a(\mathbf{k})
$$

This means the positive- and negative-frequency coefficients are not independent.

After applying this condition and relabelling the integration variable, we obtain

$$
\phi(\mathbf{x}, t) = \int\frac{d^3k}{f(k)}\left[a(\mathbf k)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x-i\omega t} +  a^*(\mathbf k)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x+i\omega t}\right]
$$

**Making the integration measure Lorentz invariant**

Define

$$
k^\mu = (k^0, \mathbf{k})
$$

$$
k^2 = k^\mu k_\mu = \mathbf{k}^2 - k_0^2
$$

For our plane-wave solutions, $k^0 = \omega$, where $\omega^2 = \mathbf{k}^2 + m^2$. Therefore,

$$
k^2 = - m^2
$$

This is the mass-shell condition.

It means that the four-momentum of a free particle of mass $m$ lies on a particular surface in four-dimensional momentum space.

**Build an invariant measure in four dimensions**

$$
d^4k \space \delta(k^2 + m^2) \space \theta(k^0)
$$

- $d^4k$ is the four-dimensional momentum-space volume element
- $\delta(k^2+m^2)$ restricts the integral to the mass shell $k^2=-m^2$
- $\theta(k^0)$ selects the positive-energy branch $k^0>0$

Consider

$$
\int_{-\infin}^{\infin} d^4k \space \delta(k^2 + m^2) \space \theta(k^0)
$$

Since, $k^2 = \mathbf{k}^2 - (k^0)^2$, the delta function is 

$$
\delta(\mathbf{k}^2 - (k^0)^2 + m^2)
$$

Its zeros occur at

$$
k^0 = \pm \sqrt{\mathbf{k}^2 + m^2} = \pm \omega
$$

The step function $\theta(k^0)$ eliminates the negative root, leaving only $k^0=\omega$.

We use the delta-function identity

$$
\delta(g(x)) = \sum_i \frac{\delta(x-x_i)}{|g'(x_i)|}
$$

where $x_i$ are the simple roots of $g(x)$.

$$
g(k^0) = \mathbf{k}^2 - (k^0)^2 + m^2, \qquad g'(k^0) = - 2 k^0
$$

At the positive root $k^0=\omega$, the absolute value of the derivative is $2\omega$. Hence,

$$
\int dk^0 \delta(k^2 + m^2) \theta(k^0) = \frac{1}{2\omega}
$$

Consequently, the invariant four-dimensional measure reduces to the invariant three-dimensional measure

$$
\boxed{\frac{d^3k}{2\omega}}
$$

We can include a conventional factor of $(2\pi)^{-3}$ and define

$$
\tilde{dk} = \frac{d^3k}{(2\pi)^3 2\omega}
$$

This is the Lorentz-invariant on-shell momentum measure.

Now choose,

$$
f(k) = (2\pi)^3 \space 2 \omega
$$

Then the field expansion becomes

$$
\phi(x) = \int \tilde{dk} \left[a(\mathbf k)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x-i\omega t} +  a^*(\mathbf k)e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x+i\omega t}\right]
$$

We have now expressed the classical field in a form using a Lorentz-invariant momentum measure.

We also invert the formula to get $a(\mathbf{k})$ in terms of $\phi(x)$. We have

$$
\int d^3x \space e^{-ikx} \space \phi(x) = \frac{1}{2 \omega} a(\mathbf{k}) + \frac{1}{2 \omega} e^{2 i \omega t} a^*(-\mathbf{k})
$$

$$
\int d^3x \space e^{-ikx} \space \partial_0 \phi(x) = - \frac{i}{2} a(\mathbf{k}) + \frac{i}{2} e^{2 i \omega t} a^*(-\mathbf{k})
$$

We can combine these to get

$$
a(\mathbf{k}) = \int d^3x \space e^{-ikx} [i \partial_0 \phi(x) + \omega \phi(x)] \\
= i \int d^3x \space e^{-ikx} \space \overleftrightarrow{\partial_0} \phi(x)
$$

where $f \overleftrightarrow{\partial_\mu} g \equiv f(\partial_\mu g) - (\partial_\mu f) g$, and $\partial_0 \phi = \partial \phi/ \partial t = \dot{\phi}$. Note that $a(\mathbf{k})$ is time independent.
