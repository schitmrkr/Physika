### Dirac Matrices

$$
H = c \alpha^j P_j + \beta mc^2
$$

Dirac's imposition on matrices

$$
\{ \alpha_j, \alpha_k \} = 2 \delta^{jk} I
$$

$$
\{ \alpha^j, \beta \} = 0
$$

$$
\beta^2 = I
$$

For an electron, we know experimentally that it has spin

$$
s = \frac{1}{2}
$$

A spin $1/2$ system has two spin states:

$$
| \uparrow \rangle, \space | \downarrow \rangle
$$

So, it is natural to think that the wave function to have two components. 

$$
\psi = \begin{pmatrix}
\psi_\uparrow \\
\psi_\downarrow
\end{pmatrix}
$$

So, matrix acting on this wave function should be $2 \times 2$. 

Pauli's matrices seem to be great candidate 

$$
\sigma^1 = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}, \space
\sigma^2 = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & -i \\
i & 0
\end{pmatrix}, \space
\sigma^3 = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 \\
0 & -1
\end{pmatrix}, \space
$$

They satisfy

$$
\{ \sigma^j, \sigma^k \} = 2 \delta^{jk} I
$$

But we also need $\beta$, and it has to satisfy

$$
\{ \alpha^j, \beta \} = 0, \space \beta^2 = I
$$

No $2 \times 2$ matrices satisfy this constraint.

The smallest possible matrices turns out to be $4 \times 4$. Therefore the Dirac wavefunction must have four components:

$$
\psi = \begin{pmatrix}
\psi_1 \\
\psi_2 \\
\psi_3 \\
\psi_4 \\
\end{pmatrix}
$$

We wanted two spin states but relativity force us to have four.

Suppose the particle has definite momentum $\mathbf{p}$. Then the momentum operators can be replaced by their eigen value.

$$
\mathbf{P} \to \mathbf{p}
$$

The Hamiltonian becomes a $4 \times 4$ matrix.

$$
H = c \alpha \cdot \mathbf{P} + \beta mc^2
$$

We already proved that 

$$
H^2 = c^2 p^2 + m^2 c^4
$$

Define

$$
E(p) = \sqrt{c^2 p^2 + m^2 c^4}
$$

Therefore,

$$
H^2 = E^2 I
$$

If $H$ has eigenvalue $\lambda$, then

$$
H \psi = \lambda \psi
$$

Apply $H$ again

$$
H^2 \psi = \lambda^2 \psi
$$

Therefore,

$$
\lambda = \pm E
$$

So Dirac's equation necessarily has both negative and positive energy solutions. The $4 \times 4$ has four eigenvalues: $+E, +E, -E, -E$. Two positive energy states and two negative energy states. The two positive states can naturally be associated with the two spin states of the electron. But what are the negative-energy states?

This is problematic because if there is an energy state $-E$, then the electron could transistion into lower energy state emitting a photon without a stable ground state. This is called negative energy problem.

Electrons obey Pauli's exclusion principle stating that two identical fermions cannot occupy exactly the same quantum state. So Dirac proposes: 

"What if every negative-energy state is already occupied by an electron?"

This hypothetical collection is called Dirac sea. The sea is the vacuum; its uniform effects are absorbed into our definition of the vacuum. Today we don't use this picture as the fundamental interpretation.

Now imagine that one negative-energy electron receives enough energy to jump into a positive-energy state. Suppose the missing electron had charge $-e$. Removing that electron leaves a net charge $+e$. So the hole behaves like a particle with positive charge. Dirac therefore predicted a new particle.

This particle is positron with the same mass as the electron and opposite electric charge. The positron was experimentally discovered by Carl Anderson in 1932.

Originally we needed a quantum equation describing one electron. But the Dirac sea picture says we need infinitely many electrons to fill all the negative-energy states. 

In ordinary quantum mechanics, $x \to \hat{x}$, but $t \to$ parameter. Time is not normally an operator like position. But special relativity says space and time are components of spacetime. So, there is an assymetry in ordinary QM. 

Klein–Gordon made the equation relativistically symmetric but the probability interpretation doesn't work like ordinary nonrelativistic quantum mechanics. Dirac fixed the first-order-time issue but then negative-energy solutions appeared. And trying to interpret those led to the Dirac sea and particle creation.

So, we keep encountering signs that:

$$ \boxed{\text{relativistic quantum mechanics is not really a single-particle theory.}} $$
