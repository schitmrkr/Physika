### Klein Gordan and Dirac Equations

$$
- \hbar \frac{\partial^2}{\partial t^2} \psi(x,t) =  (- \hbar^2 c^2 \nabla^2 + m^2 c^4) \psi(x,t)
$$

To start understanding Klein Gordon equation, we start with understanding relativity. If the coordinates $x^\mu$ represents an inertial frame, then so do any other coordinates $\bar{x}^\mu$ that are related by

$$
\bar{x}^\mu = \Alpha^\mu_\nu x^\nu + \alpha^\mu
$$

where, $\Alpha^\mu_\nu$ is Lorentz transformation matrix and $\alpha^\mu$ is a translation vector. Furthermore, $\Alpha^\mu_\nu$ must obey

$$
g_{\mu \nu} \Alpha^\mu_\rho \Alpha^\nu_\sigma = g_{\rho \sigma}
$$

This ensures that interval between two different space time points that are labelled by $x^\mu$ and $x'^\mu$ in one inertial frame, and by $\bar{x}^\mu$ and $\bar{x}'^\mu$ in another is the same. The interval is defined by

$$
(x- x')^2 = g_{\mu \nu} (x - x')^\mu (x - x')^\nu \\
= (x - x')^2 - c (t-t')^2
$$

In other frame, we have

$$
(\bar{x} - \bar{x}')^2 = g_{\mu \nu} (\bar{x} - \bar{x}')^\mu (\bar{x} - \bar{x}')^\nu \\
= g_{\mu \nu} \Alpha^\mu_\rho \Alpha^\nu_\sigma (x - x')^\rho (x - x')^\sigma \\
= g_{\rho \sigma} (x - x')^\rho (x - x')^\sigma \\
= (x- x')^2 
$$

In the case of quantum mechanics, two observers need to agree on the value of wavefunction at a particular spacetime point $x$ for one obeserver and $\bar{x}$ for another. Thus, we should have $\psi(x) = \bar{\psi}(\bar{x})$.

Lets analyse this for Klein-Gordon equation. We first introduce useful notation for spacetime derivatives:

$$
\partial_\mu = \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu} = (+\frac{1}{c} \frac{\partial}{\partial t}, \nabla)
$$

$$
\partial^\mu = \frac{\partial}{\partial x_\mu} = (-\frac{1}{c} \frac{\partial}{\partial t}, \nabla)
$$

Note that:

$$
\partial^\mu x^\nu = g^{\mu \nu}
$$

If $x$ and $\bar{x}$ are related by Lorentz tranformation equation, $\partial$ and $\bar{\partial}$ are related by

$$
\bar{\partial}^\mu = \Alpha^\mu_\nu \partial^\nu
$$

To verify this,

$$
\bar{\partial}^\rho \bar{x}^\sigma = (\Alpha^\rho_\mu \partial^\mu) (\Alpha^\sigma_\nu x^\nu + \alpha^\sigma) = \Alpha^\rho_\mu \Alpha^\sigma_\nu (\partial^\mu x^\nu) = \Alpha^\rho_\mu \Alpha^\sigma_\nu g^{\mu \nu} = g^{\rho \sigma}
$$

We can now write the Klein Gordon equation as 

$$
- \hbar^2 c^2 \partial_0^2 \psi(x) = (-\hbar^2 c^2 \nabla^2 + m^2 c^4) \psi(x)
$$

After rearranging and identifying $\partial^2 = \partial^\mu \partial_\mu = -\partial_0^2 + \nabla^2$, we have

$$
(- \partial^2 + \frac{m^2 c^2}{\hbar^2}) \psi(x) = 0
$$

Another observer would write

$$
(- \bar{\partial}^2 + \frac{m^2 c^2}{\hbar^2}) \bar{\psi}(\bar{x}) = 0
$$

To see that both of them are equivalent

$$
\bar{\partial}^2 = g_{\mu \nu} \bar{\partial}^\mu \bar{\partial}^\nu = g_{\mu \nu} \Alpha^\mu_\rho \Alpha^\nu_\sigma \partial^\rho \partial^\sigma = \partial^2
$$

The Klien Gordon equation is manifestly consistent with relativity. However Klien Gordon violates fundamental property of being first order in time derivative. One of these is that the norm of a state is not time independent. Thus probability is not conserved. The Klien-Gordon obeys relativity but not quantum mechanics.

Dirac attempted to solve this problem by introducing one discrete label on the wave function, to account for spin $\psi_a(x), \space a=1,2$. He tried a Schrodinger equation of form

$$
i \hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \psi_a(x) = (-i \hbar c (\alpha^j)_{ab} \partial_j + m c^2 (\beta)_{ab}) \psi_b(x)
$$

where all repeated indices are summed, and $\alpha^j$ and $\beta$ are matrices in spin-space. This equation, Dirac equation, is consistent with the abstract Schrodinger equation. The state $|\psi, a, t \rangle$ carries a spin label $a$ and the hamiltonian is 

$$
H_{ab} = (c P_j (\alpha^j)_{ab} + mc^2 (\beta)_{ab}) 
$$

Since Dirac equation is linear in both time and space derivative, it has a chance to be consistent with relativity. Squaring the hamilotnian yeilds

$$
(H^2)_{ab} = c^2 P_j P_k (\alpha^j \alpha^k)_{ab} + mc^3 P_j (\alpha^j \beta + \beta \alpha^j)_{ab} + (mc^2)^2 (\beta^2)_{ab}
$$

Since, $P_j P_k$ is symmetric on exchange of $j$ and $k$, we can replace $\alpha^j \alpha^k$ by its symmetric part, $\frac{1}{2}\{ \alpha^j, \alpha^k \}$, where $\{A, B\} = AB + BA$ is the anticommutator. Then if we choose matrices such that

$$
\{ \alpha^j, \alpha^k \}_{ab} = 2 \delta^{jk} \delta_{ab}, \space \{ \alpha^j, \beta \}_{ab} = 0, (\beta^2)_{ab} = \delta_{ab}
$$

we will get

$$
(H^2)_{ab} = (P^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4) \delta_{ab}
$$

Thus the Eigenstates of $H^2$ are momentum eigenstates, with $H^2$ eigenvalue $p^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4$ which is correct relativistic energy momentum relation. The Dirac equation is fully consistent with relativity given that the Dirac matrices follow the rules stated above (Out of scope to demonstrate).

**Notes**

$$
g_{\mu \nu} \Alpha^\mu_\rho \Alpha^\nu_\sigma = g_{\rho \sigma}
$$

This statement simply means that the Lorentz transformation must preserve space time interval.

Also,

$$
\partial^\mu x^\nu = g^{\mu \nu}
$$

We start with

$$
\partial_\mu x^\nu = \delta^\mu_\nu
$$

and

$$
\partial^\mu = g^{\mu \rho} \partial_\rho 
$$

Therefore,

$$
\partial^\mu x^\nu = g^{\mu \rho} \partial_\rho x^\nu \\
= g^{\mu \rho} \delta^\nu_\rho = g^{\mu \nu}
$$

Dirac's approach:

Can we construct an equation that is first order in time and also compatible with special relativity?

We start with 

$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \psi = H \psi
$$

We want to reproduce,

$$
E^2 = p^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4
$$

The naive relativistic Hamiltonian is

$$
H = \sqrt{p^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4}
$$

But this square root makes the equation mathematically awkward and non local is position space. DIrac instead propses

$$
H = c \alpha \cdot P + \beta mc^2
$$

Explicitly 

$$
H = c \alpha^1 P_1 + c \alpha^2 P_2 + c \alpha^3 P_3 + \beta mc^2
$$

Here $\alpha^1$, $\alpha^2$, $\alpha^3$, $\beta$ are matrices. This is the key new idea.

Suppose we tried 

$$
H = c P + mc^2
$$

This does not work because momentum is a vector and energy is scalar. Dirac instead introduces matrices that act on additional components of wavefunction.

So instead of $\psi(x)$ we have something like $\psi_a(x)$

$$
\psi = \begin{pmatrix}
\psi_1 \\
\psi_2 \\
\psi_3 \\
\psi_4
\end{pmatrix}
$$

These components are not simply "four independent particle"; they have a deeper relationship to spin, particle/antiparticle degrees of freedom, and Lorentz transformations.

The Dirac Hamiltonian:

$$
H = c \alpha^j P_j + \beta mc^2
$$

The Dirac equation is

$$
i\hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \psi = (c \alpha^j P_j + \beta mc^2) \psi
$$

Dirac has restored first order time evolution. But we haven't shown that it is relativistic. Relativity tells us 

$$
E^2 = p^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4 
$$

Quantum mechanically, we therefore want 

$$
H^2 = c^2 P^2 + m^2 c^4
$$

So Dirac wants to construct $H$ such that

$$
H^2 = (c^2 P^2 + m^2 c^4) I
$$

If that works, then the energy eigenvalues automatically satisfy the relativistic dispersion relation.

Now,

$$
H = c \alpha^j P_j + \beta mc^2
$$

Define $A = c \alpha^j P_j$ and $B = \beta mc^2$

$$
H^2 = A^2 + AB + BA + B^2
$$

Here,

$$
A^2 = (c \alpha^j P_j)(c \alpha^k P_k) \\ 
= c^2 \alpha^j \alpha^k P_j P_k
$$

Because momentum operator commute

$$
[P_j, P_k] = 0
$$

so,

$$
P_j P_k = P_k P_j
$$

This is why only the symmetric part of 

$$
\alpha^j \alpha^k
$$

matters. Any product can be written as 

$$
\alpha^j \alpha^k = \frac{1}{2} (\alpha^j \alpha^k + \alpha^k \alpha^j) + \frac{1}{2} (\alpha^j \alpha^k - \alpha^k \alpha^j)
$$

$$
\alpha^j \alpha^k = \frac{1}{2} \{ \alpha_j, \alpha_k \} + \frac{1}{2} [\alpha_j, \alpha_k]
$$

Now we multiply by $P_j P_k$, since it is symmetric the antisymmetric part disappears

$$
\alpha^j \alpha^k P_j P_k = \frac{1}{2} \{ \alpha_j, \alpha_k \} P_j P_k
$$

Dirac requires

$$
\{ \alpha_j, \alpha_k \} = 2 \delta^{jk} I
$$

Therefore,

$$
A^2 = c^2 \delta^{jk} P_j P_k I
$$

$$
A^2 = c^2 (P_1^2 + P_2^2 + P_3^2) I = c^2 P^2 I
$$

Now we look at,

$$
AB + BA
$$

Here,

$$
AB = c \alpha^j P_j \beta mc^2
$$

$$
BA = \beta mc^2 c \alpha^j P_j 
$$

So,

$$
AB + BA = mc^2 P_j (\alpha^j \beta + \beta \alpha^j)
$$

Now Dirac imposes

$$
\{ \alpha^j, \beta \} = 0
$$

Therefore,

$$
AB + BA = 0
$$

Now for $B^2$,

$$
B^2 = m^2 c^4 \beta^2
$$

Dirac imposes

$$
\beta^2 = I
$$

Therefore,

$$
B^2 = m^2 c^4 I
$$

Finally,

$$
H^2 = (c^2 P^2 + m^2 c^4) I
$$

And this is exactly the relativistic energy-momentum relation.
