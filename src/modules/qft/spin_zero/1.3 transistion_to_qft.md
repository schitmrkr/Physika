### Transistion to QFT

Instead of trying to make “particle position” and “time” into quantum operators on equal footing, QFT makes both space and time into labels of a field operator.

The asymmetry in the space and time can be solved with two conceptual possibilities:

- Demote position as label (which leads to QFT)
- Promote time as operator

If we treat position as label instead of operator just as we do time, the theory eventually evoloves towards Quantum Field Theory.

But if time becomes an operator, what replaces the time parameter? What is the parameter with respect to which the system evolves?

Remember Schrodinger equation:

$$
i \hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} | \psi(t) \rangle = \hat{H} | \psi(t) \rangle
$$

Now if time itself becomes an operator $t \to \hat{T}$, then the system evolves with respect to the proper time $\tau$ \(according to relativity). 

For a particle moving along a worldline,

$$
d\tau^2 = dt^2 - \frac{1}{c^2} d\mathbf{x}^2
$$

for the convention $+,-,-,-$. Proper time is measured by the clock moving with the particle. So we could say that evolution parameter is $\tau$, which means that coordinate time $T$ becomes an operator:

$$
\hat{T}(\tau)
$$

And similarly the position becomes,

$$
\hat{X}(\tau),\space \hat{Y}(\tau),\space \hat{Z}(\tau)
$$

Together we write them as

$$
\hat{X}^\mu(\tau)
$$

where,

$$
\hat{X}^0(\tau) = \hat{T}(\tau)
$$

Now spacetime coordinates are all operators.

$$
\hat{X}^\mu = (\hat{T}, \hat{X}, \hat{Y}, \hat{Z})
$$

it becomes surprisingly complicated.

The main problem is that there isn't a unique choice of evolution parameter. For example, if $\tau$ works, then

$$ \sigma=f(\tau) $$

might also work, provided $f$ is monotonic.

But this idea leads to something profound: strings. Suppose instead of 

$$
X^\mu(\tau)
$$

we may introduce another parameter $\sigma$,

$$
X^\mu(\sigma, \tau)
$$

For a point particle, $X^\mu(\tau)$ describes a world line. A particle traces out a one-dimensional curve through spacetime. 

But a string has an extension in space. As the string moves, it sweeps out a two-dimensional surface in spacetime:

$$
\boxed{\text{worldsheet}}
$$

You can think of $\sigma$ as position along string and $\tau$ as time evolution. Therefore, $X^\mu(\sigma, \tau)$ describes the string. This is essentially the starting point of string theory.

Now the QFT route says that position is not an operator $\hat{X}$ but a label $x$. Then we assign an operator to every point in space. 

For example, $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ is an operator associated with the point $\mathbf{x} = (x,y,z)$. This collection of operators is called a quantum field.

For example, a common classical field is temperature:

$$
T(\mathbf{x}, t)
$$

At every point in space and time, there is a temperature.

Similarly, an electromagnetic field:

$$
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{x}, t)
$$

At every spacetime point, there is an electric field. A quantum field is conceptually similar, except that its value is an operator.

$$
\mathbf{x} \to \phi(\mathbf{x})
$$

At each point $\mathbf{x}$, there is an operator. 

In the Heisenberg picture, operators depend on time. Therefore, 

$$
\phi(\mathbf{x}) \to \phi(\mathbf{x}, t)
$$

Here we can see that both $\mathbf{x}$ and $t$ are labels. Neither one is itself an operator. The quantum object is the field operator:

$$
\phi(\mathbf{x}, t)
$$

This is the conceptual foundation of QFT.

**QM vs QFT**

In ordinary single particle QM, we have wavefunction

$$
\psi(\mathbf{x}, t)
$$

The position operator is $\hat{X}$. A particle is the fundamental object.

But in QFT we have a field operator:

$$
\hat{\phi}(\mathbf{x}, t)
$$

The field is fundamental. Particles appear as excitations of the field. For example roughly, 

$$
\text{electron field → electron excitations,} \\
\text{photon field → photon excitations}
$$

QFT sounds completely different from ordinary quantum mechanics. But it doesn't have to be.

Start with $n$ particles. Their wavefunction is $\psi(x_1, x_2, .., x_n; t)$. For two particles

$$
\psi(x_1, x_2;t)
$$

This tells us the amplitude for finding:

- particle $1$ in $x_1$
- particle $2$ in $x_2$

The Schrödinger equation has the general form

$$
i \hbar \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \psi = \hat{H} \psi
$$

In QFT we don't really want to say: "particle 1 is here, particle 2 is there, particle 3 is there...". Instead, we want operators that can create and destroy particles at particular locations.

Introduce:

$$
a^\dagger (\mathbf{x})
$$

and

$$
a(\mathbf{x})
$$

These are called:

- $a^\dagger (\mathbf{x})$: creation operator
- $a (\mathbf{x})$: annihilation operator

What does $a^\dagger (\mathbf{x})$ do?

Define the vaccum state

$$
|0 \rangle
$$

This means no particles. Now apply the creation operator:

$$
a^\dagger (\mathbf{x}) |0 \rangle
$$

This creates one particle at $\mathbf{x}$. So,

$$
a^\dagger (\mathbf{x}) |0 \rangle = \text{-one-particle state at } \mathbf{x}
$$

Similarly,

$$
a^\dagger (\mathbf{x_1}) a^\dagger (\mathbf{x_2}) |0 \rangle 
$$

represents a state containing two particles, one at $\mathbf{x}_1$ and one at $\mathbf{x}_2$.

Why are these operators labeled by position?

Because we have assigned an operator to every point in space:

$$
\mathbf{x} \to a(\mathbf{x}) \\
\mathbf{x} \to a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}) 
$$

We have transformed the particle description into a field-like description.

For bosons, the operators satisfy crucial commutation relation

$$
[a(\mathbf{x}), a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}')] = \delta^3 (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x'})
$$

$$
[a(\mathbf{x}), a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}')] = a(\mathbf{x})a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}') - a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}') a(\mathbf{x})
$$



and

$$
[a(\mathbf{x}), a(\mathbf{x}')] = 0 \\
[a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}), a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}')] = 0
$$

This is the continuous-space version of the harmonic oscillator algebra.

For a single oscillator,

$$
[a, a^\dagger] = 1
$$

Here we have infinitely many oscillators—one associated with every spatial point:

$$
[a(\mathbf{x}), a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}')] = \delta^3 (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x'})
$$

The delta function says that creation at $\mathbf{x}$ only has the canonical relation with annihilation at the same point.

We construct the state. Now suppose we have a general one-particle wavefunction

$$
\psi(\mathbf{x}, t)
$$

We can construct the corresponding quantum state as

$$
| \psi \rangle = \int d^3 x \space \psi(x, t) a^\dagger (\mathbf{x}) |0 \rangle
$$

This says: "A particle in a spatial wavefunction $\psi(\mathbf{x})$ is a superposition of particles created at different positions."

Compare this with ordinary QM:

$$
| \psi \rangle = \int d^3 x \space \psi(x, t)  |x \rangle
$$

The identification is essentially 

$$
| x \rangle \iff a^\dagger (\mathbf{x}) |0 \rangle
$$ 

So QFT hasn't destroyed ordinary quantum mechanics. It has rewritten it.

For $n$ particles, we construct

$$
| \psi \rangle =  \int d^3 x_1 ... d^3 x_n \psi(\mathbf{x}_1, ..., \mathbf{x}_n; t) \times a^\dagger(x_1)..a^\dagger(x_n)|0 \rangle
$$

Now the wavefunction has been converted into a state in Fock space.

What is Fock space?

Ordinary $n$-particle quantum mechanics has a fixed particle number. You might have 1 particle or 2 particles but the theory is formulated for a specified $n$.

Fock space contains all particle-number sectors:

$$
\mathcal{F} = \mathcal{H}_0 \oplus \mathcal{H}_1 \oplus \mathcal{H}_2 \oplus ...
$$

where,

$$
\mathcal{H}_0 = \text{vaccum} \\
\mathcal{H}_1 = \text{one-particle states} \\
\mathcal{H}_2 = \text{two-particle states} \\
\text{...}
$$

This is what allows QFT to naturally describe particle creation and annihilation.

Griffiths introduces the number operator 

$$
N = \int d^3 x a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}) a(\mathbf{x})
$$

This is the number operator. It measures how many particles are present.

For example,

$$
N | 0 \rangle = 0
$$

For a one-particle state,

$$
N a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}) |0 \rangle = a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}) |0 \rangle
$$

For a two-particle state,

$$
N a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}_1) a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}_2) |0 \rangle = 2 a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}_1) a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}_2) |0 \rangle
$$

So the eigenvalue is the number of particles. 

**Why does $N$ commuting with $H$ matter?**

$$
[N,H]=0
$$

This means particle number is conserved.

If initially,

$$
N | \psi \rangle = n | \psi \rangle
$$

then time evolution doesn't change $n$. So,

$$
n \to n
$$

rather than,

$$
n \to n+1
$$

$$
n \to n-1
$$

Therefore this particular theory cannot describe particle creation or annihilation.

But relativistic physics needs particle creation and annihilation. This is the crucial reason QFT becomes necessary.

For example:

$$
\gamma \to e^- + e^+
$$

can occur when the appropriate conservation laws and kinematics are satisfied. 

Conversely,

$$
e^- + e^+ \to \gamma + \gamma
$$

can occur. The number of particles has changed. Ordinary fixed-$n$ quantum mechanics isn't naturally built for this. QFT is.

**Why did the Klein–Gordon/Dirac problem lead us here?**

or the Klein–Gordon equation, we encountered problems interpreting $\psi$ as an ordinary one-particle probability wavefunction. Then Dirac tried to construct a relativistic single-particle equation. But negative-energy solutions appeared:

$$
E = \pm \sqrt{p^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4}
$$

Eventually we discovered that the relativistic theory naturally involves

$$
\boxed{\text{particles + antiparticles + creation + annihilation}}
$$

That is exactly where a fixed-particle-number interpretation becomes inadequate. QFT solves this by making the field fundamental.

For bosons:

$$
[a(\mathbf{x}), a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}')] = \delta^3 (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x'})
$$

And importantly,

$$
[a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}), a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}')] = 0
$$

Therefore,

$$
a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}) a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}') = a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}') a^\dagger(\mathbf{x})
$$

The creation operators commute.

**Why does this force a symmetric wavefunction?**

Consider the two-particle state:

$$
| \psi \rangle = \int d^3 x_1 d^3 x_2 \psi(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2) a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}_1) a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}_2) | 0 \rangle
$$

Since the creation operator commutes, we get a symmetric wavefuntion. Therefore, only $\psi_S$ contributes. This gives Bose-Einstein statistics. 


**What about Fermions?**

For fermions we instead impose,

$$
\{ a(\mathbf{x}), a(\mathbf{x}') \} = 0 \\
\{ a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}), a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}') \} = 0
$$

and

$$
\{ a(\mathbf{x}), a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}') \} = \delta^3(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}')
$$

Remember:

$$
\{A,B\} = AB + BA
$$

Therefore,

$$
a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}_1) a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}_2) = - a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}_2) a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}_1) 
$$

Now the creation operators anticommute. This produces antisymmetric wavefunctions

$$
\psi(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2) = - \psi(\mathbf{x}_2, \mathbf{x}_1) 
$$

Therefore, anticommutating creation operator results in antisymmetric wavefunction which gives Fermi-Dirac statistics.

The spin-statistics theorem says, roughly:

$$
\boxed{\text{integer spin} \implies \text{boson}} 
$$

and

$$
\boxed{\text{half-integer spin} \implies \text{fermion}} 
$$

Examples

- photon $\to$ spin 1 $\to$ boson
- pion $\to$ spin 0 $\to$ boson
- Higgs $\to$ spin 0 $\to$ boson
- electron $\to$ spin 1/2 $\to$ fermion
- quark $\to$ spin 1 $\to$ fermion
- proton $\to$ spin 1 $\to$ fermion

There is an important caveat: the spin-statistics theorem is a relativistic QFT theorem under assumptions such as Lorentz invariance, locality, and a positive-definite Hilbert space.
