### Canonical Quantization of Scalar Fields

We will simplify the Hamiltonian we constructed reducing it to the hamiltonian of free particles:

$$
H = \int d^3x a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}) (- \frac{1}{2m} \nabla^2) a(\mathbf{x})
$$

$$
= \int d^3p \frac{1}{2m} \mathbf{p}^2 \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}) \tilde{a}(\mathbf{p})
$$

where,

$$
\tilde{a} (\mathbf{p}) = \int \frac{d^3 x}{(2 \pi)^{3/2}} e^{-i \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{x}} a(\mathbf{x})
$$

Here we have simplified our notation by setting

$$
\hbar = 1
$$

The appropriate factors of $\hbar$ can always be restored in any of our formulas via dimensional analysis. The commutation (or anticommutation) relations of the $\tilde{a}(\mathbf{p})$ and $\tilde{a}^\dagger (\mathbf{p})$ operators are

$$
[\tilde{a}(\mathbf{p}), \tilde{a}(\mathbf{p}')]_{\mp} = 0 \\
[\tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}), \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}')]_{\mp} = 0 \\
[\tilde{a}(\mathbf{p}), \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}')]_{\mp} = \delta^3 (\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{p}')
$$

where, $[A, B]_{\mp}$ is either the commutator (if we want a theory of bosons) or the anticommutator (if we want a theory of fermions). Thus, $\tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p})$ can be interpreted as creating a state of definite momentum $\mathbf{p}$, and the first equation describes a theory of free particles. The ground state is the vacuum $| 0 \rangle$ it is annihilated by $\tilde{a}(\mathbf{p})$,

$$
\tilde{a}(\mathbf{p}) | 0 \rangle = 0
$$

and so its energy eigenvalue is zero. The other eigenstates of $H$ are all of the form

$$
\tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p_1}) \cdots \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p_n}) | 0 \rangle
$$

and the corresponding energy eigenvalue is

$$
E(\mathbf{p}_1) + \cdots + E(\mathbf{p}_n)
$$,

where,

$$
E(\mathbf{p}) = \frac{1}{2m} \mathbf{p}^2
$$

It is easy to see how to generalize this theory to a relativistic one; all we need to do is use the relativistic energy formula $E(\mathbf{p}) = +(\mathbf{p}^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4)^{1/2}$,

$$
H = \int d^3p (\mathbf{p}^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4)^{1/2} \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}) \tilde{a}(\mathbf{p})
$$

Now we have a theory of free relativistic spin-zero particles, and they can be either bosons or fermions.

**Notes**

We start with a quantum field $a(\mathbf{x})$, Fourier transform it into momentum space $\tilde{a}(\mathbf{p})$, and discover that the field Hamiltonian is just the Hamiltonian of infinitely many independent particles/modes.

We have,

$$
H = \int d^3x a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}) (- \frac{1}{2m} \nabla^2) a(\mathbf{x})
$$

Compare this with ordinary quantum mechanics:

$$
H = \frac{\mathbf{p}^2}{2m}
$$

Since in position space (since $\hbar=1$),

$$
\mathbf{p} = - i \nabla \\
\mathbf{p}^2 = - \nabla^2
$$

Therefore,

$$
- \frac{\nabla^2}{2m} = \frac{\mathbf{p}^2}{2m}
$$

So this Hamiltonian is essentially saying: Every particle has kinetic energy $\mathbf{p}^2/(2m)$

The difference is that $a(\mathbf{x})$ is now an operator, not an ordinary wavefunction.

$$
\boxed{\text{Think of } a(\mathbf{x}) \text{ as an annihilation operator at position } \mathbf{x}}
$$

It removes a particle from position $\mathbf{x}$.

Similarly,

$$
\boxed{\text{Think of } a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}) \text{ as an creation operator at position } \mathbf{x}}
$$

So, schematically,

$$
a^\dagger(\mathbf{x}) | 0 \rangle 
$$

means: create one particle localized around $\mathbf{x}$.

$$
\boxed{\text{Position and momentum are related by Fourier transformation.}}
$$

For example, in ordinary quantum mechanics,

$$
\psi(\mathbf{x}) = \int \frac{d^3 p}{(2 \pi)^{3/2}} e^{i \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{x}} \tilde{\psi} (\mathbf{p})
$$

The same idea is applied to the operator $a(\mathbf{x})$

$$
\tilde{a}(\mathbf{p}) = \int \frac{d^3 x}{(2 \pi)^{3/2}} e^{-i \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{x}} a (\mathbf{x})
$$

and inversely,

$$
a(\mathbf{x}) = \int \frac{d^3 p}{(2 \pi)^{3/2}} e^{i \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{x}} \tilde{a}(\mathbf{p})
$$

Why does the Hamiltonian become simpler?

We have

$$
H = \int d^3 x a^\dagger(\mathbf{x})(- \frac{\nabla^2}{2m}) a(\mathbf{x})
$$

Insert the fourier expansion,

$$
a(\mathbf{x}) =  \int \frac{d^3 p}{(2 \pi)^{3/2}} e^{i \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{x}} \tilde{a}(\mathbf{p})
$$


Now consider what $\nabla^2$ does to the exponential

$$
\nabla^2 e^{i \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{x}} = - \mathbf{p}^2 e^{i \mathbf{p} \cdot \mathbf{x}}
$$

Thus,

$$
- \frac{\nabla^2}{2m} \to \frac{\mathbf{p}^2}{2m}
$$

The differential operator has become an ordinary number.

After performing the $\mathbf{x}$ integral, we obtain

$$
H = \int d^3p \frac{\mathbf{p}^2}{2m} \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}) \tilde{a}(\mathbf{p})
$$

Define the number operator for momentum $\mathbf{p}$: 

$$
N(p) = \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}) \tilde{a}(\mathbf{p})
$$

It essentially tells us how many particles occupy the momentum mode $\mathbf{p}$.

Therefore,

$$
H = \int d^3p \frac{\mathbf{p}^2}{2m} \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}) \tilde{a}(\mathbf{p})
$$

The Hamiltonian says:

$$
\boxed{\text{Total Energy = energy per particle} \times \text{number of particles}}
$$

summed over all possible momenta.

Why introduce $\tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p})$?

Suppose we start with the vacuum

$$
| 0 \rangle
$$

Apply the creation operator

$$
\tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}) | 0 \rangle
$$

This creates one particle with momentum $\mathbf{p}$.

We call this state

$$
| \mathbf{p} \rangle = \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}) | 0 \rangle
$$

That's why we interprete $\tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p})$ as a particle creation operator.

Why do he commutation relations matter?

For bosons,

$$
[\tilde{a}(\mathbf{p}), \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}')] = \delta^3(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{p}')
$$

For fermions,

$$
\{ \tilde{a}(\mathbf{p}), \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}') \} = \delta^3(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{p}')
$$

The notation,

$$
[A, B]_\mp
$$

means

$$
[A, B]_- = AB - BA
$$

for bosons, while

$$
[A, B]_+ = AB + BA
$$

for fermions.

The distinction determines the statistics of the particles.


For bosons,

$$
[\tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}), \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p'})] = 0
$$

Therefore,

$$
\tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}) \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p'}) = \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p'}) \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p})
$$

So we can create many particles in the same state.

For fermions,

$$
\{ \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}), \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}') \} = 0
$$

Putting $\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{p'}$ 

gives,

$$
2 \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p})^2 = 0 \to \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}) = 0 
$$

This is the mathematical origin of the Pauli exclusion principle.

Our Hamiltonian is 

$$
H = \int d^3p E(\mathbf{p}) \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}) \tilde{a}(\mathbf{p})
$$

where,

$$
E(\mathbf{p}) = \frac{\mathbf{p}}{2m}
$$

Act on the vacuum

$$
H | 0 \rangle = \int d^3p E(\mathbf{p}) \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}) \tilde{a}(\mathbf{p}) |0 \rangle
$$

But,

$$
\tilde{a}(\mathbf{p}) |0 \rangle = 0
$$

Therefore,

$$
H | 0 \rangle = 0
$$

This is called normal ordering / choosing the zero of energy appropriately in this simple nonrelativistic theory.

We can create $n$-particles state using the general form

$$
| \mathbf{p}_1, \cdots, \mathbf{p}_n \rangle = \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}_1) \cdots \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}_n) | 0 \rangle
$$

Its total energy is

$$
E_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i=0}^n E(\mathbf{p}_i)
$$

with

$$
E(\mathbf{p}_i) = \frac{\mathbf{p}_i}{2m}
$$

Therefore,

$$
E_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i=0}^n \frac{\mathbf{p}_i}{2m}
$$

There are no interaction terms such as $V(\mathbf{x_1} - \mathbf{x_2})$. Each particle simply carries its own energy.

In nonrelativistic mechanics,

$$
E(\mathbf{p}) = \frac{\mathbf{p}}{2m}
$$

But special relativity tells us,

$$
E^2 = \mathbf{p}^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4
$$

Therefore,

$$
E(\mathbf{p}) = \sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4}
$$

for the positive-energy branch.

If the nonrelativistic Hamiltonian counts particles weighted by their nonrelativistic energy, replace the nonrelativistic energy with the relativistic energy.

$$
H = \int d^3p \sqrt{\mathbf{p}^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4} \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p}) \tilde{a}(\mathbf{p})
$$

This Hamiltonian means exactly the same thing as before, thus the total energy is

$$
E_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i=0}^n \sqrt{\mathbf{p}_i^2 c^2 + m^2 c^4} \tilde{a}^\dagger(\mathbf{p})
$$

which is exactly the relativistic energy of a collection of free particles.

Why is this called a quantum field theory?

In ordinary quantum mechanics, you might start with:

$$
\text{one particle} \to | \mathbf{p} \rangle
$$

In quantum field theory, we instead have:

$$
\text{field} \to \text{particles emerge as excitations of the field}
$$

The central idea of QFT is:

$$
\boxed{\text{field} \to \text{particle creation/annihilation}}
$$
